I. Foundations

Gene ther­apy ad­dresses a dis­ease at the level of the gene rather than man­ag­ing its symp­toms. In prac­tice it can add a func­tional copy of a gene (gene ad­di­tion), cor­rect a mu­ta­tion di­rectly in the genome (gene edit­ing), si­lence a harm­ful gene (gene si­lenc­ing), or re­pro­gram cells to per­form a new func­tion, as in en­gi­neered-cell ther­a­pies such as CAR-T.2 The con­cep­tual case for treat­ing ge­netic dis­ease by trans­fer­ring DNA was made in the 1970s, and the first au­tho­rized human tri­als fol­lowed by 1990.1,3 Those early tri­als also de­liv­ered hard safety lessons, in­clud­ing a 1999 death from an im­mune re­sponse to an ade­n­ovi­ral vec­tor and leukemias caused by in­ser­tional mu­ta­ge­n­e­sis in an X-linked SCID trial; those events drove stronger over­sight and the safer vec­tor de­sign that un­der­lies the durable ap­provals reach­ing pa­tients since the 2010s.4,6

The two vari­ables that dom­i­nate every gene-ther­apy pro­gram are how the ge­netic pay­load is de­liv­ered and what that pay­load does once in­side the cell. De­liv­ery is ei­ther in vivo, with the vec­tor given di­rectly to the pa­tient, or ex vivo, with cells re­moved, mod­i­fied in the lab­o­ra­tory, and re­turned; the choice fol­lows the tar­get tis­sue, the mod­i­fi­ca­tion re­quired, and the avail­able tech­nol­ogy. The vec­tor plat­form then shapes be­hav­ior. Adeno-as­so­ci­ated virus (AAV) vec­tors are widely used for in vivo de­liv­ery and can drive durable ex­pres­sion, lentivi­ral vec­tors in­te­grate and sup­port sta­ble long-term ex­pres­sion in di­vid­ing and non-di­vid­ing cells and are com­mon in ex vivo cell en­gi­neer­ing, and mes­sen­ger RNA ap­proaches are in­her­ently tran­sient.2,5

For a spe­cial­ist, the re­cur­ring con­straints span cel­lu­lar ac­cess, pay­load fea­si­bil­ity, and the pa­tient's pre-ex­ist­ing im­mu­nity to the vec­tor. Off-tar­get ef­fects and the geno­tox­i­c­ity that can fol­low in­te­gra­tion or edit­ing, the man­u­fac­tur­ing process be­hind the spe­cific prod­uct, the dura­bil­ity of the ef­fect, and whether a failed or wan­ing treat­ment can be res­cued or re­peated are the prac­ti­cal ques­tions that recur across pro­grams. Dura­bil­ity in par­tic­u­lar is treat­ment, vec­tor, and prod­uct spe­cific rather than guar­an­teed. The per­sis­tence of an in vivo AAV ef­fect de­pends on the vec­tor, tar­get tis­sue, and trans­gene and has ranged from mul­ti­year sus­tained ex­pres­sion to mea­sur­able de­cline over sev­eral years, while an ex vivo ben­e­fit de­pends on whether the mod­i­fied cells en­graft and con­tinue to ex­press the ther­a­peu­tic gene. Long-term dura­bil­ity across gene ther­a­pies is still being de­fined by on­go­ing fol­low-up rather than set­tled.5

Viral Vector Platforms

Viral Vector Platforms

Viral vec­tors are the most widely used de­liv­ery ve­hi­cles for gene ther­apy, lever­ag­ing the nat­ural abil­ity of viruses to enter cells and de­liver ge­netic ma­te­r­ial. By re­mov­ing path­o­genic genes and re­plac­ing them with ther­a­peu­tic trans­genes, these en­gi­neered vec­tors can de­liver cor­rec­tive DNA or RNA to tar­get cells with high ef­fi­ciency. Each vec­tor plat­form of­fers dis­tinct ad­van­tages in terms of tro­pism, pay­load ca­pac­ity, im­muno­genic­ity, and du­ra­tion of ex­pres­sion. Man­u­fac­tur­ing and qual­ity con­sid­er­a­tions are in­te­gral to trans­lat­ing vec­tor plat­forms into safe and ef­fec­tive ther­a­pies; vec­tor prepa­ra­tions often con­tain het­ero­ge­neous species in­clud­ing func­tional and non­func­tional par­ti­cles, with po­tency assay ma­tu­rity and lot-to-lot con­sis­tency af­fect­ing dose-ef­fect pre­dictabil­ity and dura­bil­ity ex­pec­ta­tions.

CAR-T prod­ucts are ge­net­i­cally mod­i­fied T-cell ther­a­pies. 4T-VEC (Talimogene Laherparepvec / Imlygic)

IMLYGIC (tal­imo­gene la­her­parepvec) is an on­colytic HSV-1 en­gi­neered to repli­cate se­lec­tively in tumor cells and ex­press GM-CSF. In­trale­sional viral repli­ca­tion lyses in­jected tumor and re­leases tumor anti­gen plus GM-CSF to re­cruit den­dritic cells, aim­ing to prime a sys­temic anti-tumor re­sponse that can reach un­in­jected le­sions. That mech­a­nism po­si­tions it for ac­ces­si­ble cu­ta­neous, sub­cu­ta­neous, and nodal melanoma de­posits that can be di­rectly in­jected. IMLYGIC is FDA-ap­proved for the local treat­ment of un­re­sectable cu­ta­neous, sub­cu­ta­neous, and nodal le­sions in melanoma re­cur­rent after ini­tial surgery, and its la­bel­ing states that it has not been shown to im­prove over­all sur­vival or to have an ef­fect on vis­ceral metas­tases, two lim­i­ta­tions that bound how far a local re­sponse should be ex­pected to gen­er­al­ize.13 Adding sys­temic check­point in­hibitors along­side IMLYGIC has not been shown to im­prove out­comes rel­a­tive to ei­ther agent alone.

IMLYGIC la­bel­ing ad­min­is­ters the agent by in­trale­sional in­jec­tion up to 4 mL at 106 PFU/mL at the first treat­ment, then up to 4 mL at 108 PFU/mL about three weeks later and every two weeks there­after, with per-le­sion vol­umes as­signed in bands of 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, or 4 mL ac­cord­ing to the le­sion's longest di­men­sion and with le­sion pri­or­i­ti­za­tion spec­i­fied by the label. As a repli­ca­tion-com­pe­tent her­pesvirus, it car­ries label-spe­cific han­dling and im­muno­com­pro­mised-con­tact pre­cau­tions.13

5TCR-Engineered T Cells (Tecelra/Afamitresgene Autoleucel)

TECELRA shows what T-cell-re­cep­tor en­gi­neer­ing adds be­yond CAR de­sign. Its affin­ity-en­hanced re­cep­tor rec­og­nizes an in­tra­cel­lu­lar MAGE-A4 pep­tide only when pre­sented by a qual­i­fy­ing HLA-A*02 mol­e­cule, open­ing in­tra­cel­lu­lar tumor anti­gens while im­pos­ing a dual anti­gen and HLA gate. Au­tol­o­gous T cells are lentivi­rally trans­duced, ex­panded, and re­turned after lym­phode­ple­tion. The cur­rent label cov­ers pa­tients 12 years and older with un­re­sectable or metasta­tic syn­ovial sar­coma after chemother­apy, qual­i­fy­ing HLA al­le­les, and com­pan­ion-di­ag­nos­tic-con­firmed MAGE-A4 ex­pres­sion. The rec­om­mended dose is 1.62 x 109 to 10 x 109 TCR-pos­i­tive cells, and cy­tokine-re­lease syn­drome re­mains a boxed-warn­ing risk.33 It is given as a sin­gle in­tra­venous in­fu­sion in one or more bags, with each thawed bag ad­min­is­tered within 1 hour.33 Be­cause recog­ni­tion de­pends on both anti­gen and HLA, can­cer-testis anti­gen ex­pres­sion and HLA pre­sen­ta­tion must be demon­strated di­rectly in the in­tended tumor and pa­tient pop­u­la­tion, so the ap­proval val­i­dates this prod­uct and in­di­ca­tion rather than a can­cer-testis anti­gen class or a re­pro­grammed-cell plat­form.33

6Engineered and Nonengineered Autologous Cell-Therapy Comparison

TECELRA and AMTAGVI solve dif­fer­ent recog­ni­tion prob­lems. TECELRA adds a de­fined HLA-re­stricted re­cep­tor against an in­tra­cel­lu­lar MAGE-A4 pep­tide, pro­duc­ing a stan­dard­ized tar­get gate but lim­it­ing treat­ment to com­pat­i­ble HLA and anti­gen-pos­i­tive tu­mors. AMTAGVI ex­pands nat­u­rally oc­cur­ring tumor-in­fil­trat­ing lym­pho­cytes from a re­sected melanoma with­out in­tro­duc­ing a re­cep­tor, pre­serv­ing poly­clonal tumor recog­ni­tion but mak­ing tis­sue ac­qui­si­tion and ex­pan­sion part of the prod­uct. Both carry boxed-warn­ing risks and re­quire lym­phode­ple­tion; the ap­plic­a­ble label for each prod­uct states its bound­aries. Their human-scale ori­en­ta­tion is prod­uct-spe­cific by cell iden­tity and route. En­gi­neered TECELRA is dosed at 1.62 × 109 to 10 × 109 MAGE-A4 TCR-pos­i­tive T cells once in­tra­venously, whereas nonengi­neered AMTAGVI is dosed at 7.5 × 109 to 72 × 109 vi­able tumor-de­rived T cells once in­tra­venously in 1 to 4 bags. AMTAGVI adds sup­port­ive IL-2 be­gin­ning 3 to 24 hours after in­fu­sion at 600,000 IU/kg every 8 to 12 hours for up to 6 doses.33,34

Gene Therapy for Inherited Disorders

In­her­ited ge­netic dis­or­ders, caused by mu­ta­tions in sin­gle genes (mono­genic dis­eases) or de­fined ge­netic loci, rep­re­sent ideal tar­gets for gene ther­apy. These con­di­tions have well-char­ac­ter­ized mol­e­c­u­lar pathol­ogy, iden­ti­fi­able pa­tient pop­u­la­tions, and mea­sur­able clin­i­cal end­points. Gene ther­apy ap­proaches in­clude gene ad­di­tion (de­liv­er­ing a func­tional gene copy), gene cor­rec­tion (edit­ing the mu­tant gene in situ), and gene mod­u­la­tion (al­ter­ing ex­pres­sion of mod­i­fier genes to com­pen­sate for the pri­mary de­fect). El­i­gi­bil­ity and ex­pected ben­e­fit are de­fined per prod­uct in each ther­apy's la­bel­ing and trial ev­i­dence rather than by a gen­eral dis­ease-stage rule; mol­e­c­u­lar con­fir­ma­tion, tis­sue-spe­cific de­liv­ery con­straints, and dis­ease sever­ity are con­sid­er­a­tions ad­dressed within each prod­uct's own in­di­ca­tion.

1AAV-OTOF (Otoferlin Gene Therapy for DFNB9 Deafness)

OTARMENI uses two AAV1 vec­tors to de­liver the large OTOF cod­ing se­quence in com­ple­men­tary parts to cochlear inner hair cells. Otofer­lin re­stores synap­tic vesi­cle re­lease at the hair-cell and au­di­tory-nerve in­ter­face, so pre­served outer hair-cell func­tion and sur­gi­cally ac­ces­si­ble inner-ear anatomy de­fine the bi­o­log­i­cal win­dow. FDA granted ac­cel­er­ated ap­proval in 2026 for se­vere-to-pro­found or pro­found hear­ing loss caused by con­firmed bial­lelic OTOF vari­ants. Each ear re­ceives 7.2 x 1012 vec­tor genomes in 0.24 mL by in­tra­cochlear in­fu­sion, and bi­lat­eral treat­ment may occur in one sur­gi­cal ses­sion, with surgery, cor­ti­cos­teroid pro­phy­laxis, and menin­gi­tis pre­ven­tion in­te­gral to the prod­uct ar­chi­tec­ture.35

2Beta-Thalassemia Gene Therapy

ZYNTEGLO (betibeglo­gene au­totem­cel) is the lentivi­ral gene-ad­di­tion route for trans­fu­sion-de­pen­dent beta-tha­lassemia, re­duc­ing the trans­fu­sion bur­den by adding a func­tional beta-glo­bin gene rather than edit­ing an en­doge­nous locus. It con­sists of au­tol­o­gous CD34-pos­i­tive cells trans­duced ex vivo with the BB305 lentivi­ral vec­tor en­cod­ing beta-A-T87Q glo­bin, and is ap­proved for adult and pe­di­atric pa­tients who re­quire reg­u­lar red-blood-cell trans­fu­sions, most of whom (32 of 36 evalu­able) achieved trans­fu­sion in­de­pen­dence in the piv­otal pop­u­la­tion. Be­cause it is an in­te­grat­ing lentivi­ral prod­uct, the label re­quires hema­to­logic-ma­lig­nancy mon­i­tor­ing at least an­nu­ally for at least 15 years, a prod­uct-spe­cific bound­ary that is not trans­fer­able to the CRISPR route. The min­i­mum rec­om­mended dose is 5.0 x 106 CD34+ cells/kg by sin­gle in­tra­venous in­fu­sion, sup­plied at a prod­uct con­cen­tra­tion of 2.0 to 20 x 106 cells/mL in up to four bags; the tar­get aphere­sis col­lec­tion is at least 12 x 106 CD34+ cells/kg, with a backup of at least 1.5 x 106 CD34+ cells/kg. For the same trans­fu­sion-de­pen­dent in­di­ca­tion, CASGEVY pro­vides the al­ter­na­tive ex-vivo CRISPR route de­scribed above.14,15

3Cystic Fibrosis Gene Therapy

Cys­tic fi­bro­sis is a com­pelling nu­cleic-acid tar­get be­cause restor­ing CFTR ex­pres­sion could work across many mu­ta­tion classes, but air­way de­liv­ery must over­come mucus, in­flam­ma­tion, ep­ithe­lial turnover, in­nate sens­ing, and the need for re­peat dos­ing. MRT5005 tested the tran­sient-re­place­ment the­sis with neb­u­lized CFTR mRNA in lipid nanopar­ti­cles. In 42 adults it pro­duced febrile and hy­per­sen­si­tiv­ity re­ac­tions and no con­sis­tent FEV1 ben­e­fit. The re­sult does not in­val­i­date CFTR re­place­ment. It shows that de­liv­ery, tol­er­a­bil­ity, and re­peat­able ep­ithe­lial ex­pres­sion, rather than tar­get se­lec­tion alone, are the gov­ern­ing bot­tle­necks.16

MRT5005 tested neb­u­lized lipid-nanopar­ti­cle CFTR mRNA under sep­a­rate de­signs: sin­gle 8, 16, 20, or 24 mg co­horts; once-weekly 8, 12, 16, or 20 mg co­horts for five doses; and 4 mg daily for five days. These ex­po­sures de­scribe one dis­con­tin­ued in­ves­ti­ga­tional for­mu­la­tion and do not form a gen­eral in­haled mRNA range.48,49

4RDEB (Recessive Dystrophic Epidermolysis Bullosa)

Re­ces­sive dy­s­trophic epi­der­mol­y­sis bul­losa (RDEB) is caused by mu­ta­tions in COL7A1 en­cod­ing type VII col­la­gen (C7), lead­ing to se­vere blis­ter­ing and chronic wounds. Mul­ti­ple gene ther­apy ap­proaches are under in­ves­ti­ga­tion.

B-VEC (berema­gene geper­pavec), an HSV-1 vec­tor de­liv­er­ing COL7A1, achieved FDA ap­proval on the basis of a ran­dom­ized Phase 3 trial show­ing sig­nif­i­cant wound clo­sure ver­sus placebo, with an open-label ex­ten­sion sup­port­ing dura­bil­ity. Mar­keted as VYJUVEK, it is the top­i­cally ap­plied, re­peat­ably dosed route, with weekly top­i­cal ceil­ings of 2 × 109 PFU below age 3 and 4 × 109 PFU at age 3 or older and per-wound tiers of 4 × 108 to 1.2 × 109 PFU.55

EB-101, au­tol­o­gous ker­atinocyte sheets gene-cor­rected with retro­vi­ral COL7A1, re­ported durable wound heal­ing in a ran­dom­ized Phase 3 trial and reached FDA ap­proval as pradema­gene za­mik­er­a­cel, mar­keted as ZEVASKYN. It is the sur­gi­cally ap­plied au­tol­o­gous cell-sheet route, in which one sheet cov­ers 41.25 cm2 and up to 12 gene-mod­i­fied cel­lu­lar sheets may be man­u­fac­tured per pa­tient.54 RDEB there­fore now has two ap­proved routes with dis­tinct nu­mer­i­cal ori­en­ta­tions: the re­peat­ably dosed top­i­cal VYJUVEK and the sin­gle-ap­pli­ca­tion ZEVASKYN cell sheets.

Lentivi­ral fi­brob­last gene ther­apy using COL7A1-mod­i­fied au­tol­o­gous fi­brob­lasts re­ported safety and type VII col­la­gen restora­tion in an early-phase trial and re­mains in­ves­ti­ga­tional, and non-viral poly­mer (HPAE) de­liv­ery of COL7A1 shows only pre­clin­i­cal promise in RDEB mouse graft mod­els.

5Alzheimer's Disease Gene Therapy Concepts

LX1001 uses in­trathe­cal AAVrh.10 de­liv­ery of APOE2 cDNA to shift the cen­tral ner­vous sys­tem of APOE4 ho­mozy­gotes to­ward an APOE2 and APOE4 pro­tein en­vi­ron­ment. The ap­proach in­tro­duces mod­i­fier-gene re­place­ment rather than cor­rect­ing the in­her­ited APOE4 al­le­les. It could alter lipid trans­port, amy­loid han­dling, or down­stream neu­ro­bi­ol­ogy, but dif­fuse brain dis­tri­b­u­tion, dose-lim­ited vec­tor ex­po­sure, ir­re­versible de­liv­ery, dis­ease-stage se­lec­tion, and the need to sep­a­rate bio­marker con­ver­sion from cog­ni­tive ben­e­fit are the pri­mary un­re­solved bot­tle­necks. The par­ent study and long-term fol­low-up es­tab­lish an early safety and tar­get-en­gage­ment pro­gram, not clin­i­cal ef­fi­cacy.24,36

LX1001 il­lus­trates cen­tral ner­vous sys­tem vec­tor scal­ing. Its first three in­tracis­ter­nal co­horts used 1.4 × 1010, 4.4 × 1010, or 1.4 × 1011 vec­tor genomes per mL of mea­sured cere­brospinal fluid, fol­lowed by a fixed 1.4 × 1014 total-vec­tor-genome co­hort. These are early, study-spe­cific ex­po­sures rather than an in­tracis­ter­nal AAV range.50

6Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) Gene Therapy Concepts

CKD gene ther­apy ap­proaches tar­get ge­netic vari­ants dri­ving pro­gres­sive renal dys­func­tion. APOL1 (apolipopro­tein L1) gain-of-func­tion risk vari­ants (G1 and G2) cause a toxic nephropa­thy pre­dom­i­nantly in in­di­vid­u­als of African an­ces­try, and the ther­a­peu­tic con­cept is to si­lence the toxic gain-of-func­tion al­le­les by an­ti­sense-oligonu­cleotide or CRISPR-based sup­pres­sion while pre­serv­ing nor­mal APOL1 func­tion. This re­mains an in­ves­ti­ga­tional mech­a­nis­tic con­cept rather than a val­i­dated pa­tient-se­lec­tion or mon­i­tor­ing pro­to­col.

Al­port syn­drome gene ther­a­pies tar­get COL4A3, COL4A4, or COL4A5 (base­ment mem­brane col­la­gen genes) caus­ing pro­gres­sive hered­i­tary nephri­tis; podocyte-lin­eage-spe­cific cor­rec­tion via AAV-me­di­ated in vivo gene de­liv­ery or ex vivo gene-cor­rected podocyte dif­fer­en­ti­a­tion from pa­tient-de­rived in­duced pluripo­tent stem cells (iPSCs) are under pre­clin­i­cal and early clin­i­cal de­vel­op­ment. COL4A mu­ta­tions cause base­ment mem­brane dis­rup­tion and pro­gres­sive glomeru­lar in­jury; early in­ter­ven­tion be­fore ex­ten­sive scar­ring may im­prove out­comes.

The APOL1 and Al­port-syn­drome gene-ther­apy ap­proaches re­main in­ves­ti­ga­tional, and the Al­port-syn­drome data re­main at bioRxiv preprint stage with­out peer-re­viewed pub­li­ca­tion. AZD2373, the human APOL1 si­lenc­ing pro­gram, used six weekly sub­cu­ta­neous ad­min­is­tra­tions across undis­closed low, medium, and high dose co­horts; no equiv­a­lent nu­mer­i­cal human gene-trans­fer dose has been iden­ti­fied for Al­port syn­drome.

7Heart Failure Gene Therapy Concepts

Heart fail­ure gene ther­apy strate­gies tar­get ge­netic or ac­quired car­diac dys­func­tion through fi­bro­sis pre­ven­tion, cal­cium-han­dling restora­tion, and struc­tural pro­tein cor­rec­tion. SOX9 (SRY-box tran­scrip­tion fac­tor 9) dele­tion in en­dothe­lial cells pre­vents car­diac fi­bro­sis across mul­ti­ple dis­ease mod­els (trans­verse aor­tic con­stric­tion, an­giotensin II in­fu­sion, is­chemic in­jury); SOX9 nor­mally pro­motes en­dothe­lial-to-mes­enchy­mal tran­si­tion (EndMT), and its sup­pres­sion pre­serves en­dothe­lial in­tegrity while re­duc­ing fi­brotic sig­nal­ing. CRISPR-me­di­ated SOX9 ab­la­tion or an­ti­sense oligonu­cleotide-me­di­ated sup­pres­sion ap­proaches are in pre­clin­i­cal de­vel­op­ment.

TTN trun­cat­ing vari­ants are a major ge­netic cause of di­lated car­diomy­opa­thy and rarely, if ever, cause hy­per­trophic car­diomy­opa­thy. CRISPR-based exon-skip­ping or readthrough ap­proaches re­main pre­clin­i­cal, and no human ther­a­peu­tic reg­i­men is es­tab­lished.25

LMNA (lamin A/C) mu­ta­tions cause in­flam­ma­tory-fi­brotic car­diomy­opa­thy via im­paired nu­clear in­tegrity and sus­tained NF-kB ac­ti­va­tion; gene ther­a­pies com­bin­ing LMNA cor­rec­tion with NF-kB path­way mod­u­la­tion (see Gene Ther­apy IV, Emerg­ing and Spe­cial­ized Ap­proaches, Sec­tion 3, Path­way Pre­con­di­tion­ing and Syn­thetic Gene Cir­cuits) are con­cep­tu­ally ra­tio­nal.

CUPID 2 was a ran­dom­ized Phase 2b trial of in­tra­coro­nary AAV1/SERCA2a in heart fail­ure with re­duced ejec­tion frac­tion. At the tested dose, it did not im­prove time to re­cur­rent heart-fail­ure events ver­sus placebo. The study does not es­tab­lish phe­no­type-spe­cific ef­fi­cacy or a re­quire­ment for ad­junc­tive anti-in­flam­ma­tory ther­apy, and its re­sult stands as a neg­a­tive Phase 2b human trial.26

CUPID 2 ad­min­is­tered MYDICAR once at 1 × 1013 DNase-re­sis­tant par­ti­cles in 50 mL of nor­mal saline by in­tra­coro­nary in­fu­sion over 10 min­utes. Its neu­tral phase 2b out­come makes this a study-spe­cific AAV1/SERCA2a ex­po­sure, not a plat­form reg­i­men.51,52

IV. Emerging and Specialized Approaches

Emerging Platforms

Emerging Platforms and Next-Generation Approaches

Emerg­ing gene-ther­apy plat­forms dif­fer by cargo, de­liv­ery route, tar­get tis­sue, ex­pres­sion du­ra­tion, and whether they edit DNA, reg­u­late RNA, or mod­ify cells. In vivo edit­ing, RNA-based ap­proaches, and en­gi­neered vec­tors there­fore re­quire plat­form-spe­cific ev­i­dence and bound­aries.

  1. Di­rect Cell Re­pro­gram­ming (Trans­d­if­fer­en­ti­a­tion)

Di­rect re­pro­gram­ming uses lin­eage-defin­ing fac­tors to move a so­matic cell di­rectly into an­other func­tional lin­eage with­out first cre­at­ing a pluripo­tent in­ter­me­di­ate. Three fac­tors con­verted mouse fi­brob­lasts into elec­tri­cally ac­tive, synapse-form­ing neu­rons in vitro, while Gata4, Mef2c, and Tbx5 con­verted res­i­dent car­diac fi­brob­lasts into car­diomy­ocyte-like cells after local de­liv­ery in in­farcted mouse hearts, where the in­farcted my­ocardium re­ceived 10 mi­cro­liters of mixed retro­virus plus 10 mi­cro­liters of PBS.49,50 The de­sign choice avoids a pluripo­tent-cell man­u­fac­tur­ing stage by con­vert­ing res­i­dent so­matic cells into a needed lin­eage di­rectly, which sup­ports ei­ther ex vivo dis­ease-model gen­er­a­tion or in situ tis­sue re­place­ment.

Trans­la­tion turns on lin­eage fi­delity, func­tional mat­u­ra­tion, con­ver­sion ef­fi­ciency, per­sis­tence of fac­tor ex­pres­sion, and tis­sue-spe­cific de­liv­ery. The neu­ronal and car­diac stud­ies are dis­tinct mouse proofs of con­cept, not one shared plat­form.49,50 No ad­min­is­tered human di­rect-re­pro­gram­ming prod­uct was iden­ti­fied.

  1. eNOS-EPCs (En­gi­neered En­dothe­lial Prog­en­i­tor Cells)

Over­ex­press­ing en­dothe­lial ni­tric oxide syn­thase in en­dothe­lial prog­en­i­tor cells raises local ni­tric-oxide bioavail­abil­ity and im­proves the vas­cu­lar-re­pair ca­pac­ity of prog­en­i­tor cells, a ra­tio­nale sup­ported in pre­clin­i­cal vas­cu­lar-in­jury mod­els.39 The ap­proach is most rel­e­vant in is­chemic or en­dothe­lial-dys­func­tion set­tings where prog­en­i­tor func­tion is im­paired. Trans­la­tion de­pends on prod­uct-spe­cific iden­tity, en­graft­ment, and dura­bil­ity, none yet es­tab­lished in hu­mans. A reg­is­tered human pul­monary ar­te­r­ial hy­per­ten­sion study de­liv­ered in­cre­men­tal eNOS-trans­fected EPC doses through a pul­monary-artery line over three days, and the pub­lic reg­istry does not ex­pose exact cell counts; pre­clin­i­cal eNOS en­dothe­lial-like prog­en­i­tor stud­ies used ap­prox­i­mately 1 × 106 to 1.5 × 106 cells in­tra­venously in rat PAH mod­els.39

  1. En­gi­neered AAV Cap­sids

Cap­sid en­gi­neer­ing is the en­abling layer that de­cides whether an AAV pay­load reaches its tar­get tis­sue at an ac­cept­able dose and im­muno­genic­ity, po­si­tion­ing it as a cross-cut­ting in­flu­ence over down­stream gene-ther­apy ap­pli­ca­tions rather than as a ther­apy for any sin­gle dis­ease. Di­rected evo­lu­tion, struc­ture-guided de­sign, and ma­chine-learn­ing-guided de­sign all aim to im­prove tis­sue tro­pism, man­u­fac­tur­ing yield, and eva­sion of pre-ex­ist­ing neu­tral­iz­ing an­ti­bod­ies. Mus­cle-tropic vari­ants such as MyoAAV show the pay­off, achiev­ing markedly en­hanced skele­tal and car­diac trans­duc­tion rel­a­tive to nat­ural serotypes across species,34 and AAVrh74, the cap­sid used in the FDA-ap­proved Duchenne ther­apy Ele­v­idys, demon­strates that a mus­cle-di­rected cap­sid can an­chor an ap­proved prod­uct; Ele­v­idys is dosed once in­tra­venously at 1.33 × 1014 vg/kg for pa­tients below 70 kg or a fixed 9.31 × 1015 vg at 70 kg or more.35

The lim­it­ing trans­la­tion vari­able is species-spe­cific cap­sid-re­cep­tor bi­ol­ogy. AAV-PHP.B achieves en­hanced cen­tral-ner­vous-sys­tem trans­duc­tion only in per­mis­sive mouse strains, through the mouse LY6A re­cep­tor whose ex­pres­sion varies by strain, and this in­ter­ac­tion does not trans­fer to species that lack it.41,42 As an en­abling plat­form rather than an ad­min­is­tered prod­uct, cap­sid en­gi­neer­ing car­ries no work­ing dose range of its own; its pa­ra­me­ters are set by the spe­cific vec­tor and pro­gram in which a cap­sid is de­ployed.

  1. Ex­o­some-Me­di­ated Gene De­liv­ery

En­gi­neered ex­tra­cel­lu­lar vesi­cles could offer a mod­u­lar, cell-de­rived car­rier that ac­cepts un­like nu­cleic-acid pay­loads with­out chang­ing the cargo's bi­o­log­i­cal ob­jec­tive. In one 2024 plat­form study, mul­ti­va­lent cationic lipid en­gi­neer­ing en­abled vesi­cles to load plas­mid DNA, mRNA, or siRNA, and the for­mu­la­tions sup­ported in vitro trans­fec­tion or re­porter knock­down in tar­get cells.40 That breadth mat­ters be­cause one car­rier con­cept could sup­port tran­sient pro­tein ex­pres­sion, gene ad­di­tion, or gene si­lenc­ing.

The same ex­per­i­ment de­fines the bound­ary. It was an in vitro en­gi­neer­ing demon­stra­tion, not ev­i­dence of tis­sue-se­lec­tive de­liv­ery, in vivo biodis­tri­b­u­tion, im­mune com­pat­i­bil­ity, scal­able man­u­fac­tur­ing, or clin­i­cal dos­ing. Ex­tra­cel­lu­lar-vesi­cle source, sur­face chem­istry, cargo load­ing, pu­rity, po­tency, and tar­get-cell up­take there­fore re­main prod­uct-defin­ing vari­ables, and no clin­i­cal work­ing range can be trans­ferred from this study. In that in vitro work, the plas­mid-DNA for­mu­la­tion used 1 × 1011 EV par­ti­cles/mL with pDNA at 1.6 mi­cro­grams/mi­cro­liter, the mRNA for­mu­la­tions used 0.4 to 3 mi­cro­grams mRNA per 1 × 1011 par­ti­cles, and the siRNA for­mu­la­tion used 1 nmol per well with 10 mi­cro­grams MVL5 per 1 × 1010 par­ti­cles; no ad­min­is­tered human ther­a­peu­tic dose has been iden­ti­fied.40

  1. In Situ CAR Gen­er­a­tion

In situ CAR gen­er­a­tion aims to pro­gram a pa­tient's T cells in­side the body, re­plac­ing leuka­phere­sis, ex vivo viral trans­duc­tion, ex­pan­sion, and re­in­fu­sion with tar­geted de­liv­ery of tran­sient or durable ge­netic in­struc­tions. Tar­geted lipid nanopar­ti­cles car­ry­ing CAR mRNA have re­pro­grammed se­lected T-cell pop­u­la­tions in pre­clin­i­cal mod­els, and a 2025 clin­i­cal re­port de­scribed in vivo CD19 CAR-T gen­er­a­tion in re­frac­tory sys­temic lupus ery­the­mato­sus. In that five-pa­tient HN2301 re­port, 2 or 4 mg was ad­min­is­tered in­tra­venously one to three times at two-day in­ter­vals with­out lym­phode­ple­tion, defin­ing a prod­uct-spe­cific stud­ied ex­po­sure rather than an in situ CAR class range.52 The plat­form's dis­tin­guish­ing fea­ture is man­u­fac­tur­ing by­pass and pos­si­ble re­peata­bil­ity. The un­re­solved con­trol prob­lem is whether de­liv­ery speci­ficity, trans­fec­tion frac­tion, ex­pres­sion du­ra­tion, dose es­ca­la­tion, and off-tar­get im­mune ac­ti­va­tion can be mea­sured tightly enough with­out an ex vivo re­lease gate.51,52

  1. In Vivo CRISPR Gene Edit­ing

In vivo CRISPR edit­ing de­liv­ers edit­ing com­po­nents di­rectly to tar­get or­gans, re­mov­ing the need for ex vivo cell ma­nip­u­la­tion and trans­plan­ta­tion. In­tel­lia's NTLA-2001 (nex­ig­u­ran zi­clumeran) pro­vided the land­mark proof of con­cept, an LNP-de­liv­ered CRISPR ther­apy tar­get­ing transthyretin in he­pa­to­cytes for hered­i­tary transthyretin amy­loi­do­sis. In the first-in-human re­port, sin­gle in­tra­venous doses of 0.1 or 0.3 mg/kg total RNA were stud­ied, and mean serum TTR fell by about 87 per­cent at the 0.3 mg/kg dose by day 28,36 and a later phase 1 car­diomy­opa­thy co­hort re­ported re­duc­tions of roughly 90 per­cent sus­tained at 12 months, es­tab­lish­ing durable in vivo edit­ing in hu­mans.37 A com­pan­ion pro­gram, NTLA-2002, tar­gets KLKB1 for hered­i­tary an­gioedema, using sin­gle in­tra­venous doses of 25, 50, or 75 mg in phase 1 and 25 or 50 mg in phase 2, and re­duced at­tack rates in a ran­dom­ized trial.38 Ad­di­tional in vivo liver-di­rected edit­ing pro­grams are in de­vel­op­ment, ex­pand­ing from the ini­tial rare-mono­genic-dis­ease proofs of con­cept to­ward a broader in­di­ca­tion range; any spe­cific pro­gram is de­scribed only from its own cited source, dose, tar­get, and fol­low-up.

Re­peat ad­min­is­tra­tion of an LNP-de­liv­ered ed­i­tor, if con­sid­ered, re­quires prod­uct-spe­cific eval­u­a­tion of de­liv­ery, edit­ing, im­muno­genic­ity, and safety; the ab­sence of AAV cap­sid-spe­cific im­mu­nity does not by it­self es­tab­lish re­dos­ing safety or ef­fec­tive­ness.3 Safety rea­son­ing ben­e­fits from sep­a­rat­ing mea­sure­ment from mean­ing, be­cause high-ac­cu­racy off-tar­get pre­dic­tion or vari­ant call­ing does not by it­self es­tab­lish bi­o­log­i­cal con­se­quence or clin­i­cal ac­tion­abil­ity, and treat­ing those lay­ers as equiv­a­lent is a com­mon error in ge­nomic safety as­sess­ment.

Which in­di­ca­tions are reach­able is bounded by ge­nomic ac­ces­si­bil­ity of the tar­get locus, base­line dis­ease stage, pre-ex­ist­ing im­mu­nity to Cas9 or the de­liv­ery ve­hi­cle, and tis­sue tro­pism, and dual-com­po­nent Cas9-plus-guide sys­tems re­quire co­or­di­nated man­u­fac­tur­ing and po­tency as­sess­ment. These pro­gram-spe­cific doses do not de­fine a gen­eral in vivo CRISPR range.

Targeted Oligonucleotide Delivery Comparators

An­ti­sense oligonu­cleotides and small-in­ter­fer­ing RNAs act at RNA but dif­fer in mech­a­nism and de­liv­ery. ASOs can alter splic­ing, re­cruit RNase H, or block trans­la­tion and may reach the cen­tral ner­vous sys­tem through in­trathe­cal ad­min­is­tra­tion. GalNAc con­ju­ga­tion in­stead ex­ploits the he­pa­to­cyte asialo­gly­co­pro­tein re­cep­tor to in­ter­nal­ize se­lected siRNAs after sub­cu­ta­neous dos­ing. The com­par­i­son be­longs at the de­liv­ery level. Nusin­ersen demon­strates re­peated in­trathe­cal ac­cess to motor neu­rons, while vutrisiran demon­strates re­cep­tor-me­di­ated liver tar­get­ing.53,54

Cur­rent sched­ules show how route and con­ju­ga­tion change the de­liv­ery prob­lem. SPINRAZA uses ei­ther a 12 mg in­trathe­cal load­ing and four-month main­te­nance se­quence or the cur­rent high-dose se­quence of two 50 mg doses 14 days apart fol­lowed by 28 mg every four months. AMVUTTRA uses 25 mg sub­cu­ta­neously every three months. These label-spe­cific prod­uct com­para­tors do not form a class-wide dose or sched­ule range.58,59

Oligonucleotide-Antibody Conjugates (AOCs)

Oligonu­cleotide-an­ti­body con­ju­gates use an an­ti­body as an ac­tive tis­sue-de­liv­ery de­vice rather than merely as a car­rier. Del­pacibart et­edesiran cou­ples an anti-trans­fer­rin-re­cep­tor-1 an­ti­body to siRNA against DMPK, using re­cep­tor-me­di­ated up­take to reach skele­tal and car­diac mus­cle in my­otonic dy­s­tro­phy type 1. Re­cep­tor-me­di­ated mus­cle up­take ex­tends RNA si­lenc­ing be­yond the liver, where GalNAc con­ju­ga­tion dom­i­nates cur­rent ap­proved ap­proaches. The open ques­tions on trans­la­tion in­clude tis­sue ex­po­sure, en­do­so­mal es­cape, linker and pay­load sta­bil­ity, re­cep­tor bi­ol­ogy, and whether mol­e­c­u­lar cor­rec­tion pro­duces func­tional ben­e­fit. Phase 3 reg­is­tra­tion marks clin­i­cal ma­tu­rity, not ap­proval or a class-wide reg­i­men.5,55

MARINA stud­ied del­pacibart et­edesiran as one 1 mg/kg in­tra­venous ex­po­sure or three in­tra­venous ex­po­sures at 2 or 4 mg/kg in my­otonic dy­s­tro­phy type 1, and the phase 3 HARBOR trial ad­min­is­ters 4 mg/kg in­tra­venously every eight weeks.5 The co­horts be­long to that trans­fer­rin-re­cep­tor-1 an­ti­body and DMPK-siRNA con­ju­gate and can­not be trans­ferred across an­ti­body-oligonu­cleotide plat­forms.60

mRNA Therapeutics

mRNA en­ables pro­gram­ma­ble, tran­sient pro­tein ex­pres­sion with­out ge­nomic in­te­gra­tion. Its de­sign space is gov­erned by se­quence op­ti­miza­tion, mod­i­fied nu­cle­o­sides, un­trans­lated re­gions, cap and poly(A) ar­chi­tec­ture, de­liv­ery ve­hi­cle, tar­get tis­sue, and the tol­er­ated du­ra­tion of ex­pres­sion. MRESVIA shows that an mRNA-lipid-nanopar­ti­cle prod­uct can be ap­proved for vac­ci­na­tion, while in­di­vid­u­al­ized neoanti­gen vac­cines test pa­tient-spe­cific anti­gen en­cod­ing and MRT5005 shows the pul­monary-de­liv­ery chal­lenge. N1-methyl­pseudouri­dine can re­duce in­nate sens­ing and im­prove trans­la­tion, but se­quence-de­pen­dent ri­bo­so­mal frameshift­ing shows that im­mune eva­sion and trans­la­tional fi­delity must be op­ti­mized to­gether.6,56,57

Route and scale dif­fer sharply by prod­uct. MRESVIA de­liv­ers a sin­gle 50-mi­cro­gram in­tra­mus­cu­lar dose of nu­cle­o­side-mod­i­fied mRNA in 0.5 mL,6 in­haled MRT5005 for CFTR was stud­ied at 4 to 24 mg across sin­gle, weekly, or five-day de­signs, and tar­geted in­tra­venous HN2301 de­liv­ers 2 or 4 mg of CAR mRNA per ad­min­is­tra­tion across one to three ad­min­is­tra­tions.52

  1. Ya­manaka Fac­tor Cell Re­pro­gram­ming

The four Ya­manaka fac­tors (Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc) re­pro­gram mouse fi­brob­lasts to pluripo­tency.43 Clin­i­cal de­vel­op­ment cen­ters on par­tial or tran­sient re­pro­gram­ming, which aims to re­store as­pects of youth­ful cell func­tion with­out eras­ing cell iden­tity.44

The gov­ern­ing con­straints are safety and con­trol. In nat­u­rally aged mice, a sin­gle cycle of tran­sient re­pro­gram­ming re­versed some mark­ers of aging with­out chang­ing cell iden­tity,44 but re­pro­gram­ming-depth con­trol re­mains the cen­tral un­solved prob­lem, be­cause there is no es­tab­lished bio­marker for the bound­ary be­tween ben­e­fi­cial par­tial re­pro­gram­ming and loss of iden­tity or tu­mori­genic ded­if­fer­en­ti­a­tion, and onco­genic risk is not lim­ited to c-Myc. Cyclic OSKM in­duc­tion of two days on and five days off for three weeks has been used as the safe par­tial-re­pro­gram­ming sched­ule in mice,44 whereas over eight weeks in an­i­mals car­ry­ing two OSKM cas­sette copies the same cyclic in­duc­tion pro­duced in­creased cel­lu­lar pro­lif­er­a­tion and ter­atomas, so no human OSKM sched­ule can be in­ferred from these mod­els. Par­tial re­pro­gram­ming re­mains in­ves­ti­ga­tional. It es­tab­lishes no clin­i­cal reg­i­men, safe re­pro­gram­ming-depth bound­ary, epi­ge­netic-age thresh­old, or gen­eral de­liv­ery ap­proach, and any human study must be de­scribed only from its di­rectly cited reg­istry or pro­to­col.

The first human par­tial-re­pro­gram­ming study, ER-100, is a Phase 1 trial giv­ing one in­trav­it­real ad­min­is­tra­tion to one eye at 2 × 1011 or 6 × 1011 vec­tor genomes per eye in the glau­coma dose-es­ca­la­tion co­hort, with sys­temic doxy­cy­cline for 8 weeks to ac­ti­vate OSK ex­pres­sion. It plans up to 18 par­tic­i­pants with fol­low-up through Year 5. These are in­ves­ti­ga­tional pro­to­col pa­ra­me­ters, not ef­fi­cacy ev­i­dence, and be­cause no de­fined prod­uct or ad­min­is­tra­tion sched­ule ex­ists as a class, no work­ing range ap­plies.65

Investigational Gene Therapies (Non-FDA Approved)

The en­tries in this sec­tion are in­ves­ti­ga­tional gene ther­a­pies that are not FDA-ap­proved; each is de­scribed only from its cited reg­istry or pri­mary record.

  1. AAV-Fol­lis­tatin Gene Ther­apy (Myo­statin In­hi­bi­tion)

AAV-fol­lis­tatin trans­fer of­fers a durable, po­ten­tially sin­gle-ad­min­is­tra­tion source of a myo­statin an­tag­o­nist, re­duc­ing re­peated dos­ing to one in­ter­ven­tion and mak­ing it a can­di­date for both age-re­lated mus­cle de­cline and in­her­ited neu­ro­mus­cu­lar dis­ease. The ev­i­dence is early and does not iso­late that ef­fect. A Phase 1/2a study in Becker mus­cu­lar dy­s­tro­phy re­ported het­ero­ge­neous in­di­vid­ual re­sults across six par­tic­i­pants,7 and the study now reg­is­tered for age-re­lated mus­cle de­cline com­bines AAV-fol­lis­tatin with a VEGF plas­mid, a de­sign that can­not at­tribute any ob­served change to fol­lis­tatin rather than to the co-ad­min­is­tered vec­tor and trans­gene.8

The de­liv­ery plat­form sets bound­aries the con­cept has to re­spect, and their sever­ity is vec­tor-, tis­sue-, dose-, and prod­uct-de­pen­dent rather than fixed. Pre-ex­ist­ing anti-AAV neu­tral­iz­ing an­ti­bod­ies can re­duce or pre­clude ef­fi­cacy, and re­dos­ing is often con­strained be­cause durable anti-vec­tor im­mu­nity tends to fol­low a sys­temic ex­po­sure, though im­mune-priv­i­leged tis­sues can be an ex­cep­tion. Vec­tor tro­pism shapes biodis­tri­b­u­tion and off-tar­get up­take, and a sta­bly ex­pressed trans­gene can make ex­pres­sion dif­fi­cult to re­verse, so the dura­bil­ity and re­versibil­ity of any given prod­uct must be judged for its spe­cific vec­tor, tis­sue, and dose rather than as­sumed.48 Re­ported en­roll­ment and dose lev­els are pro­to­col-de­fined re­search pa­ra­me­ters rather than a clin­i­cal work­ing range.7,8

In a Becker mus­cu­lar dy­s­tro­phy study, AAV1.CMV.FS344 was de­liv­ered to bi­lat­eral quadri­ceps at 3 × 1011 or 6 × 1011 vec­tor genomes/kg per leg. These are named human-study ex­po­sures for that vec­tor, con­struct, mus­cle map, and im­mune-mon­i­tor­ing ap­proach.7

Klotho Gene Delivery (Plasmid and mRNA, Investigational)

Alpha-Klotho is a kid­ney-de­rived cir­cu­lat­ing and mem­brane pro­tein whose mea­sured lev­els de­cline with age, whose loss pro­duces mul­ti­sys­tem aging-like phe­no­types in knock­out mod­els, and whose lower lev­els in peo­ple are as­so­ci­ated with car­dio­vas­cu­lar, renal, and neu­rode­gen­er­a­tive dis­ease, which to­gether make durable restora­tion of Klotho an at­trac­tive longevity and organ-pro­tec­tion tar­get.46 Gene de­liv­ery is being tested through two dis­tinct early clin­i­cal ap­proaches, plas­mid DNA and mRNA, which should not be pooled as one ge­netic-med­i­cine modal­ity.9,10

Both ap­proaches re­main in­ves­ti­ga­tional. Plas­mid DNA is epi­so­mal and gen­er­ally tran­sient, re­duc­ing in­ser­tional con­cern but also work­ing against a dura­bil­ity the­sis. mRNA is also tran­sient and shifts the de­sign prob­lem to­ward organ-tar­geted de­liv­ery and re­peat dos­ing. ClinicalTrials.gov lists a small non-placebo-con­trolled plas­mid study as re­cruit­ing and an alpha-Klotho mRNA lipid-nanopar­ti­cle Phase 1 study as not yet re­cruit­ing at their lat­est posted up­dates, with no ef­fi­cacy, dos­ing, organ-out­come, or other re­sults posted for ei­ther pro­gram. Mea­sured Klotho ex­po­sure, tis­sue dis­tri­b­u­tion, du­ra­tion, and de­fined renal or car­diometa­bolic end­points are re­quired to sep­a­rate gene-de­liv­ery fea­si­bil­ity from ther­a­peu­tic ef­fect.9,10 The plas­mid study used a sin­gle sub­cu­ta­neous in­jec­tion into ab­dom­i­nal fat across 24 planned par­tic­i­pants; the pub­lic reg­istry dis­closes no plas­mid mass, con­cen­tra­tion, vol­ume, or mRNA-LNP dose for ei­ther pro­gram.

  1. Telom­erase Gene Ther­apy (hTERT)

AAV-hTERT gene de­liv­ery aims to rein­tro­duce telom­erase to counter the telom­ere short­en­ing that lim­its replica­tive lifes­pan, and its cen­tral con­cern is that this same ca­pa­bil­ity sits on a rec­og­nized can­cer path­way. Telom­erase re­ac­ti­va­tion is a ma­lig­nancy-en­abling hall­mark in most human can­cers, be­cause re­stored replica­tive ca­pac­ity is one of the bar­ri­ers a tumor must over­come, so sus­tained hTERT ex­pres­sion raises a real onco­genic­ity con­cern that any re­ju­ve­na­tion ra­tio­nale has to weigh, even though telom­erase ac­tiv­ity by it­self is not a trans­form­ing event and does not es­tab­lish a treat­ment-at­trib­ut­able can­cer rate.47

No ad­min­is­tered human AAV-hTERT dose has been re­ported pub­licly. The reg­is­tered study has un­known sta­tus and no posted re­sults.11 The avail­able nu­mer­i­cal an­chor, one tail-vein AAV9-mTERT ex­po­sure of 2 × 1012 vec­tor genomes per mouse in aged C57BL/6-back­ground an­i­mals, de­fines only the tested con­struct and model; it can­not be con­verted into a human start­ing dose.64 Any human pro­gram would re­quire prod­uct-spe­cific vec­tor-genome es­ca­la­tion and pro­longed on­co­logic sur­veil­lance be­cause the in­tended telom­ere-main­te­nance ef­fect and the prin­ci­pal onco­genic con­cern arise from the same bi­o­log­i­cal axis.

  1. VEGF Gene Ther­apy (Neo­vas­cul­gen / An­gio­gen­e­sis)

VEGF gene trans­fer at­tempts to cre­ate a local, sus­tained an­gio­genic sig­nal in is­chemic tis­sue rather than re­peat­edly ad­min­is­ter­ing a short-lived pro­tein. The con­cept is bi­o­log­i­cally co­her­ent but clin­i­cally un­for­giv­ing be­cause ves­sel growth must be spa­tially use­ful, ma­ture, per­fused, and com­pat­i­ble with the un­der­ly­ing tis­sue. In a ran­dom­ized placebo-con­trolled se­vere-angina trial, in­tramy­ocar­dial plas­mid VEGF-A165 did not im­prove the pri­mary my­ocar­dial-per­fu­sion end­point. This con­trolled neg­a­tive re­sult demon­strates that trans­gene ex­pres­sion and an­gio­gen­e­sis do not au­to­mat­i­cally pro­duce func­tional per­fu­sion. Other VEGF plas­mid pro­grams re­main spe­cific to each prod­uct and in­di­ca­tion.45,12

The Neo­vas­cul­gen phase 2b/3 pro­gram used the su­per­coiled pCMV-VEGF165 plas­mid in­tra­mus­cu­larly at 1.2 mg twice, 14 days apart, in ath­er­o­scle­rotic lower-limb is­chemia. This is a prod­uct and in­di­ca­tion-bound ex­po­sure rather than a VEGF gene-ther­apy range.61,62

Pathway Preconditioning

Pathway Preconditioning and Synthetic Gene Circuits

Path­way pre­con­di­tion­ing is cell-state en­gi­neer­ing rather than a trans­fer­able drug recipe. NRF2 and NF-kB cou­pling can re­verse di­rec­tion across ex­per­i­men­tal con­texts, mTOR in­hi­bi­tion can bias mem­ory CD8 T-cell dif­fer­en­ti­a­tion, and the tim­ing of NF-kB ac­ti­va­tion can de­ter­mine whether la­tent en­hancers open.13,14,16 Path­way in­puts should there­fore be judged by the phe­no­type and po­tency of the re­sult­ing man­u­fac­tured cells, not by the path­way label alone.

Each process must re­solve the rel­e­vant cell source, stim­u­lus, ex­po­sure tim­ing, re­sult­ing dif­fer­en­ti­a­tion state, and prod­uct po­tency be­fore a path­way in­ter­ven­tion can be treated as ben­e­fi­cial. These stud­ies do not pro­vide class-wide con­cen­tra­tions or sched­ules, and their sig­nals should not be trans­ferred to AAV, lipid-nanopar­ti­cle, or en­gi­neered-cell pro­grams with­out prod­uct-spe­cific test­ing.13,14,16 Syn­thetic cir­cuits are a dif­fer­ent en­gi­neer­ing layer be­cause they add de­signed sens­ing and re­sponse logic rather than merely shift­ing the start­ing cell state.17

1PI3K-Delta/Gamma Inhibition in CAR-T Manufacturing

Ex vivo PI3K-delta/gamma in­hi­bi­tion dur­ing man­u­fac­ture of CAR-T cells de­rived from pa­tients with chronic lym­pho­cytic leukemia en­riched sev­eral less dif­fer­en­ti­ated CD8 phe­no­types and im­proved ac­tiv­ity in ex­per­i­men­tal as­says and mod­els. In the re­ported process, 300 nM du­velisib was re­plen­ished every three days across a 14-day cul­ture. This is process-bound ex vivo ex­po­sure, not a pa­tient dose or a com­mon man­u­fac­tur­ing range. The sep­a­rate bb21217 pro­gram dis­closed use of bb007 dur­ing cul­ture but not its iden­tity, con­cen­tra­tion, or a final-prod­uct washout spec­i­fi­ca­tion.15,63

2Programmable Antigen-Sensing Circuits

Syn­thetic Notch-to-CAR cir­cuits have pro­duced con­di­tional CAR ex­pres­sion and dual-anti­gen dis­crim­i­na­tion in pri­mary human T cells and mouse tumor mod­els. This is di­rect pre­clin­i­cal proof of pro­gram­ma­ble cel­lu­lar logic, with clin­i­cal safety, dura­bil­ity, and re­sis­tance to anti­gen evo­lu­tion un­re­solved.17

The pri­mary in-vivo demon­stra­tion used a tightly de­fined xenograft sys­tem rather than a plat­form-wide cell dose. In NSG mice with paired CD19-pos­i­tive and GFP/CD19-dou­ble-pos­i­tive K562 xenografts, 2 × 106 total pri­mary human CD4-pos­i­tive and CD8-pos­i­tive T cells car­ry­ing an anti-GFP synNotch re­cep­tor linked to in­ducible anti-CD19 CAR ex­pres­sion were given in­tra­venously four days after tumor im­plan­ta­tion, and tumor growth was fol­lowed for 20 days.17 That is a cir­cuit-spe­cific pre­clin­i­cal ex­po­sure, not a clin­i­cal ad­min­is­tra­tion range.

Stem Cell Immortalization

Stem Cell Immortalization and Manufacturing Strategies

Im­mor­tal­iza­tion can re­duce the replica­tive lim­its of a re­search or pro­ducer cell line while also chang­ing geno­type, phe­no­type, dif­fer­en­ti­a­tion, growth con­trol, and tu­mori­genic po­ten­tial; ev­i­dence must be in­ter­preted at the level of the spe­cific cell line, con­struct, pas­sage his­tory, man­u­fac­tur­ing process, and final prod­uct. A con­di­tion­ally im­mor­tal­ized neural stem-cell line using a c-mycERTAM con­struct was eval­u­ated in the phase 1 PISCES study after chronic is­chemic stroke; that first-in-human safety study does not es­tab­lish a gen­eral c-mycERTAM man­u­fac­tur­ing or ther­a­peu­tic pro­to­col, and its find­ings be­long to that spe­cific cell line and in­di­ca­tion.18

1Mesenchymal Stromal Cell Telomerase Extension

hTERT-mod­i­fied mes­enchy­mal stro­mal cells re­main re­search cell lines rather than a val­i­dated ther­a­peu­tic class. One ex­ten­sively char­ac­ter­ized bone-mar­row line re­tained sev­eral pri­mary-cell fea­tures and was non­tu­mori­genic in the re­ported tests after pro­longed cul­ture. A dif­fer­ent hTERT-trans­duced MSC model ac­quired ad­di­tional mol­e­c­u­lar changes and be­came tu­mori­genic at late pas­sage. The con­trast demon­strates why hTERT ex­pres­sion alone can­not sup­port a class-wide safety or equiv­a­lence claim.19,20 The non­tu­mori­genic bone-mar­row line was char­ac­ter­ized through 155 pop­u­la­tion dou­blings, while the sep­a­rate hMSC-TERT20 work de­rived tu­mori­genic clones at pop­u­la­tion dou­bling 440; no ad­min­is­tered human ther­a­peu­tic cell dose has been iden­ti­fied for ei­ther line.

Peptide-Nucleic Acid Conjugates

Peptide-Nucleic Acid Conjugates for Gene Therapy

Pep­tide-oligonu­cleotide con­ju­gates com­bine a nu­cleic-acid cargo with a pep­tide in­tended to im­prove up­take, tis­sue tar­get­ing, in­tra­cel­lu­lar traf­fick­ing, or func­tional de­liv­ery. Re­ported ex­am­ples in­clude cell-pen­e­trat­ing pep­tide-mor­pholino con­ju­gates21,22 and re­cep­tor-tar­geted pep­tide-an­ti­sense sys­tems.23,24 GalNAc is a car­bo­hy­drate lig­and rather than a pep­tide and is not a pep­tide-nu­cleic-acid con­ju­gate, and an­ti­body-oligonu­cleotide con­ju­gates are a sep­a­rate modal­ity.1,2,4,5

Pre­clin­i­cal stud­ies show both po­ten­tial and con­straint. Pip6a-PMO up­take and exon-skip­ping ac­tiv­ity dif­fered among mouse skele­tal-mus­cle cells and car­diomy­ocytes, with sub­stan­tial vesic­u­lar ac­cu­mu­la­tion and per­sis­tent en­do­so­mal-es­cape lim­i­ta­tions.21 DG9-PMO im­proved exon skip­ping, dy­s­trophin restora­tion, and se­lected func­tional mea­sures in a hu­man­ized DMD mouse model.22 Re­cep­tor-tar­geted his­ti­dine-rich pep­tide con­ju­gates im­proved splice cor­rec­tion in re­cep­tor-ex­press­ing cul­tured cells,23 while imag­ing of a GLP-1-ASO con­ju­gate showed that linker sta­bil­ity changed the rel­a­tive sub­cel­lu­lar dis­tri­b­u­tion of pep­tide and oligonu­cleotide.24 These find­ings are chem­istry-, re­cep­tor-, cell-, and model-spe­cific.

ClinicalTrials.gov lists the phase 2 vesleteplirsen study25 and the phase 2 PGN-EDO51 study26 as ter­mi­nated; reg­istry sta­tus alone does not es­tab­lish the rea­son or a class-wide safety or ef­fi­cacy con­clu­sion for ei­ther plat­form.

Func­tional de­liv­ery re­quires more than total cel­lu­lar up­take. Con­ju­gate sta­bil­ity, re­cep­tor den­sity, en­do­so­mal traf­fick­ing, cy­toso­lic or nu­clear re­lease, cargo in­tegrity, tis­sue ex­po­sure, tox­i­c­ity, and im­muno­genic­ity must be mea­sured sep­a­rately. No uni­ver­sal en­do­so­mal-es­cape per­cent­age or pre­ferred pep­tide ar­chi­tec­ture is es­tab­lished by the cur­rent ev­i­dence.21–26

Pep­tide-con­ju­gated oligonu­cleotides use cell-pen­e­trat­ing or tis­sue-di­rected pep­tides to im­prove up­take of oth­er­wise poorly in­ter­nal­ized nu­cleic acids. SRP-5051's pep­tide-phos­pho­ro­di­ami­date mor­pholino con­ju­gate sought stronger exon 51 skip­ping in Duchenne mus­cu­lar dy­s­tro­phy, but de­vel­op­ment stopped after pro­longed hy­po­mag­ne­semia and de­clines in es­ti­mated glomeru­lar fil­tra­tion rate. That prod­uct-spe­cific human re­sult con­strains pep­tide, linker, tis­sue-ex­po­sure, and renal-han­dling de­sign with­out es­tab­lish­ing a class-wide ver­dict.33

Cancer Cell Reprogramming

Cancer Cell Reprogramming as Antigen Platforms

Can­cer-cell re­pro­gram­ming, tumor-anti­gen dis­cov­ery, and ther­a­peu­tic anti­gen tar­get­ing are dis­tinct re­search lay­ers. Each anti­gen claim re­quires di­rect demon­stra­tion of pre­sen­ta­tion, tumor se­lec­tiv­ity, nor­mal-tis­sue ex­clu­sion, im­muno­genic­ity, and prod­uct-spe­cific safety.29–32

1Induced Stem-Like Cancer Cell Reprogramming

Mouse melanoma cells have been re­pro­grammed through nu­clear trans­fer or de­fined tran­scrip­tion fac­tors into pluripo­tent or pluripo­tent-like states. These stud­ies show that ma­lig­nant genomes can re­main de­vel­op­men­tally plas­tic, not that re­pro­gram­ming re­moves onco­genic risk or re­pro­duces a pa­tient's tumor anti­gen land­scape. In the nu­clear-trans­fer model, the re­pro­grammed genome still pre­dis­posed de­rived an­i­mals to ma­lig­nancy after onco­genic sig­nal­ing was re­stored; a pre­clin­i­cal chal­lenge with 5 × 106 re­pro­grammed or con­trol cells im­planted sub­cu­ta­neously in SCID mice pro­duced tu­mors within ap­prox­i­mately 2 to 3 weeks under those in­duc­tion con­di­tions.27,28

2Noncanonical Peptide Antigens from Small Open Reading Frames

Pro­teoge­nomic stud­ies iden­tify HLA-as­so­ci­ated pep­tides from non­canon­i­cal read­ing frames across tumor datasets, ex­pand­ing the dis­cov­ery space be­yond an­no­tated pro­teins. Dis­cov­ery is not equiv­a­lent to a us­able tar­get. One study test­ing pa­tient-de­rived tumor lines found no recog­ni­tion of its tested non­canon­i­cal lig­ands by au­tol­o­gous ex­panded tumor-re­ac­tive T cells, even though other anti­gen classes were rec­og­nized. Can­di­date se­lec­tion there­fore re­quires or­thog­o­nal spec­trum val­i­da­tion, HLA bind­ing, nor­mal-tis­sue ex­clu­sion, and func­tional T-cell test­ing.31,32

V. Frameworks and Reference Tools

1. Introduction

1. Introduction

Gene-ther­apy pro­grams share de­sign and man­u­fac­tur­ing prob­lems that cut across in­di­vid­ual vec­tors and prod­ucts. This chap­ter con­nects CAR-T ar­chi­tec­ture and tox­i­c­ity, tar­geted-pro­tein degra­da­tion, pep­tide-as­sisted de­liv­ery, pre-vec­tor val­i­da­tion, bio­marker in­ter­pre­ta­tion, and cur­rent US man­u­fac­tur­ing con­trols so that the same con­straint can be com­pared across plat­forms.

2. Class Pharmacology

2. Class Pharmacology and Mechanism Frameworks

These en­tries de­scribe class-wide phar­ma­col­ogy and mech­a­nism find­ings for gene ther­apy rather than a sin­gle named prod­uct. They cover CAR-T ar­chi­tec­tural, man­u­fac­tur­ing, and tox­i­c­ity bi­ol­ogy, and PROTAC tar­geted pro­tein degra­da­tion as a com­ple­men­tary modal­ity to gene si­lenc­ing.

CAR-T Architecture, Manufacturing, and Toxicity Framework

Each chimeric anti­gen re­cep­tor T-cell prod­uct is de­fined by its tar­get anti­gen, bind­ing do­main, hinge and trans­mem­brane el­e­ments, sig­nal­ing do­mains, vec­tor or edit­ing sys­tem, source cells, man­u­fac­tur­ing process, re­lease at­trib­utes, in­di­ca­tion, and treat­ment con­text. Gen­er­a­tion la­bels and in­di­vid­ual de­sign el­e­ments de­scribe a con­struct; they do not pre­dict ef­fi­cacy, per­sis­tence, or tox­i­c­ity across prod­ucts, which is why FDA guid­ance re­quires prod­uct-spe­cific chem­istry, man­u­fac­tur­ing, con­trol, non­clin­i­cal, and clin­i­cal jus­ti­fi­ca­tion for each one.1 Re­cep­tor en­gi­neer­ing and T-cell ac­ti­va­tion are mech­a­nis­ti­cally well un­der­stood, but that con­fi­dence does not trans­fer to cross-prod­uct clin­i­cal pre­dic­tion. Iden­tity, ef­fi­cacy, and risk must re­main bound to the in­di­vid­ual prod­uct.

That prod­uct-level speci­ficity has di­rect clin­i­cal con­se­quences. Piv­otal sin­gle-arm stud­ies es­tab­lished ac­tiv­ity for ti­s­agen­le­cleu­cel in pe­di­atric and young-adult re­lapsed or re­frac­tory B-cell acute lym­phoblas­tic leukemia and for axi­cab­ta­gene ciloleu­cel in re­frac­tory large B-cell lym­phoma, but those re­sults can­not be pooled into a class-wide re­sponse, sur­vival, or dura­bil­ity es­ti­mate, and cur­rent in­di­ca­tions, warn­ings, ad­min­is­tra­tion, and mon­i­tor­ing re­quire­ments must be read from the cur­rent prod­uct la­bel­ing.2–5 Man­u­fac­tur­ing is equally re­sis­tant to gen­er­al­iza­tion. Au­tol­o­gous work­flows com­monly move through col­lec­tion, cell se­lec­tion or en­rich­ment, ac­ti­va­tion, ge­netic mod­i­fi­ca­tion, ex­pan­sion, test­ing, re­lease, ship­ping, and ad­min­is­tra­tion, but the se­quence, con­trols, ac­cep­tance cri­te­ria, fresh or cry­op­re­served state, and tim­ing vary by prod­uct, so a generic step count or vein-to-vein du­ra­tion should never be pre­sented as a spec­i­fi­ca­tion or a pa­tient promise.

The most con­se­quen­tial bound­aries are the tox­i­c­i­ties. Cy­tokine re­lease syn­drome and im­mune ef­fec­tor cell-as­so­ci­ated neu­ro­tox­i­c­ity syn­drome are se­ri­ous, prod­uct-la­beled risks. A clin­i­cal study of adults treated with a de­fined CD19 CAR-T prod­uct linked se­vere neu­ro­tox­i­c­ity to en­dothe­lial ac­ti­va­tion and blood-brain bar­rier dis­rup­tion, a mech­a­nis­ti­cally in­for­ma­tive find­ing that nonethe­less does not es­tab­lish a uni­ver­sal bio­marker thresh­old or a portable treat­ment al­go­rithm; grad­ing, pro­phy­laxis, treat­ment, mon­i­tor­ing, and res­cue fol­low the cur­rent prod­uct label and the in­sti­tu­tion's gov­erned clin­i­cal pro­to­col.6 Fail­ure after CAR-T is sim­i­larly mul­ti­fac­to­r­ial, re­flect­ing anti­gen per­sis­tence, anti­gen loss, cell fit­ness, ex­po­sure, tumor con­text, and host bi­ol­ogy. Dual-tar­get, logic-gated, ar­mored, al­lo­geneic, and in vivo de­signs are de­vel­op­ment strate­gies spe­cific to the con­struct and in­di­ca­tion, not a cross-prod­uct se­lec­tion or res­cue al­go­rithm.

PROTACs sit at the edge of this frame­work as a re­lated but dis­tinct modal­ity. They are tar­geted pro­tein-degra­da­tion con­structs, not a CAR-T man­u­fac­tur­ing step or an es­tab­lished gene-ther­apy in­te­gra­tion mech­a­nism. Pep­tide-based de­graders and nu­cleic-acid or cell ther­a­pies may be stud­ied within one de­vel­op­ment pro­gram, but no gen­eral clin­i­cal com­bi­na­tion, AAV-en­coded PROTAC-pep­tide strat­egy, or cross-modal­ity pro­to­col is es­tab­lished. Ad­min­is­tered pa­ra­me­ters be­long to the in­di­vid­ual ap­proved prod­ucts and their la­bels. Head-to-head, prod­uct-re­solved long-term data on dura­bil­ity and de­layed tox­i­c­ity would be needed to move from prod­uct-by-prod­uct cau­tion to­ward com­par­a­tive se­lec­tion logic.

3. Peptide-Assisted Delivery…

3. Peptide-Assisted Delivery Platforms and Pre-Vector Validation

Pep­tide tools can lower de­vel­op­ment risk be­fore a pro­gram com­mits to viral-vec­tor man­u­fac­tur­ing. VEGF-de­rived and FGF-de­rived pep­tide frag­ments let a pro­gram probe tar­get bind­ing and path­way di­rec­tion in ex­per­i­men­tal sys­tems, and cell-pen­e­trat­ing pep­tides are stud­ied as car­ri­ers that might im­prove en­do­so­mal es­cape for nu­cleic-acid cargo. Both are de­vel­op­ment aids, not val­i­dated bridges to a vec­tor prod­uct.

  1. VEGF-De­rived Pep­tide Frag­ments as Pre-Vec­tor Tar­get Val­i­da­tion Tools

VEGF-de­rived and VEGF-re­cep­tor-tar­get­ing pep­tides can let a gene-ther­apy team probe bind­ing and sig­nal­ing be­fore com­mit­ting to vec­tor man­u­fac­tur­ing. They are lower-cost tar­get-val­i­da­tion and assay-de­vel­op­ment tools, not a val­i­dated bridge from pep­tide dose re­sponse to vec­tor dose, ex­pres­sion dura­bil­ity, tis­sue biodis­tri­b­u­tion, or a clin­i­cal ther­a­peu­tic win­dow.

The sup­port­ing ev­i­dence is spe­cific and bounded. Syn­thetic twelve-amino-acid pep­tides en­coded by VEGF-A exon 6 in­hib­ited VEGF re­cep­tor bind­ing and VEGF-de­pen­dent en­dothe­lial re­sponses in the re­ported in vitro sys­tems, and later work al­tered the VEGF 125-136 se­quence and eval­u­ated re­cep­tor bind­ing, cell as­says, and la­beled-pep­tide imag­ing in tumor mod­els.8–10 In that later work, op­ti­mized pep­tides reached IC50 val­ues of 80 and 185 nmol/L in a VEGF re­cep­tor com­pe­ti­tion screen span­ning 0, 1.3, 6.5, 32.5, 65, 650, and 6,500 nmol/L, more po­tent than the 464 nmol/L mea­sured for VEGF 125-136,9 and a ra­di­o­la­beled pep­tide was given to tumor-bear­ing mice at 4 mi­cro­grams with 18.5 MBq per an­i­mal in­tra­venously on days 1 and 7 as a pre­clin­i­cal ra­dio­ther­a­peu­tic demon­stra­tion rather than a human pep­tide or vec­tor dose.10 These re­sults sup­port a tar­get-prob­ing and mol­e­c­u­lar-probe con­cept; they do not show that the pep­tides re­pro­duce full-length VEGF sig­nal­ing or pre­dict a gene-ther­apy prod­uct. Struc­tural map­ping of VEGF re­cep­tor-bind­ing de­ter­mi­nants and en­gi­neered VEGF mim­ics fur­ther shows that pep­tide ac­tiv­ity de­pends on se­quence, con­for­ma­tion, va­lency, and assay con­text, so a re­cep­tor-de­rived or lig­and-mimetic pep­tide may an­tag­o­nize sig­nal­ing through com­pet­i­tive or non­com­pet­i­tive mech­a­nisms that must be demon­strated for each con­struct rather than in­ferred from se­quence ori­gin.7,11

The same cau­tion ap­plies to FGF-di­rected pep­tides. A phage-se­lected basic-FGF-bind­ing pep­tide in­hib­ited basic-FGF-dri­ven pro­lif­er­a­tion and neo­vas­cu­lar­iza­tion in the tested cell and chick-mem­brane as­says, which is an an­tag­o­nist ex­am­ple, not ev­i­dence that an un­de­fined FGF-21 vari­ant re­pro­duces FGF sig­nal­ing or es­tab­lishes a pre-vec­tor ther­a­peu­tic win­dow.12 In every case the pep­tide an­swers a nar­row ques­tion about tar­get en­gage­ment, path­way di­rec­tion, assay re­spon­sive­ness, and struc­ture-ac­tiv­ity re­la­tion­ships, while vec­tor fea­si­bil­ity de­pends sep­a­rately on ex­pres­sion cas­sette, dose, route, cell type, pro­tein pro­cess­ing, spa­tial ex­po­sure, du­ra­tion, im­mu­nity, biodis­tri­b­u­tion, and tox­i­col­ogy. Be­cause these are re­search tools rather than ad­min­is­tered prod­ucts, no work­ing range ap­plies, and no pep­tide-to-vec­tor con­ver­sion fac­tor or go-for­ward thresh­old should be in­ferred. No prospec­tive pro­gram has yet demon­strated that a pep­tide-de­rived tar­get-en­gage­ment read­out pre­dicts a down­stream vec­tor out­come; that demon­stra­tion would be needed be­fore these tools can serve as val­i­dated pre-vec­tor bridges.

  1. Cell-Pen­e­trat­ing Pep­tides as Nu­cleic Acid Cargo De­liv­ery Vec­tors

Cell-pen­e­trat­ing pep­tides promise a so­lu­tion to one of the hard­est prob­lems in nu­cleic-acid ther­a­peu­tics by mov­ing an in­tact, func­tional cargo out of the en­do­some and into the cy­tosol or nu­cleus. That promise keeps them an ac­tive de­liv­ery-re­search plat­form, and the key dis­tinc­tion is be­tween up­take and func­tional de­liv­ery. They are a het­ero­ge­neous set of cationic, am­phi­pathic, or hy­dropho­bic se­quences stud­ied as co­va­lent con­ju­gates or non­co­va­lent car­ri­ers. Early ex­per­i­ments re­ported cel­lu­lar up­take of pen­e­tratin, TAT-de­rived pep­tides, Trans­portan, and pVEC car­gos, but later com­par­a­tive work showed that up­take and lo­cal­iza­tion vary with pep­tide se­quence, cargo, cell type, con­cen­tra­tion, and assay, so flu­o­res­cent or total-cell up­take can­not be equated with in­tact cargo reach­ing its site of ac­tion.

Pri­mary oligonu­cleotide stud­ies make the dis­tinc­tion con­crete. TAT, pen­e­tratin, R9, and Trans­portan con­ju­gates en­tered cy­toso­lic com­part­ments in cul­tured cells, yet the freely de­liv­ered oligonu­cleotides did not enter the nu­cleus or pro­duce the in­tended tran­scrip­tional ef­fect with­out a cationic-lipid aid.13 Pip6a-PMO drove full exon skip­ping at 250 nM after 24 hours in cell cul­ture, with an EC50 of 41 nM across a tested range of 15 to 500 nM, and its ac­tiv­ity dif­fered be­tween skele­tal-mus­cle cells and car­diomy­ocytes in as­so­ci­a­tion with dis­tinct traf­fick­ing and vesic­u­lar re­ten­tion,17 while re­cep­tor-tar­geted his­ti­dine-rich pep­tides im­proved func­tional splice cor­rec­tion in a de­fined re­cep­tor-pos­i­tive cell model, show­ing that tar­get­ing and es­cape func­tions can be com­bined but not gen­er­al­ized.18 De­sign de­pen­dence runs through the pVEC work as well. The orig­i­nal pep­tide car­ried a short pep­tide-nu­cleic-acid cargo and a model pro­tein into cul­tured cells, while later se­quence mod­i­fi­ca­tions al­tered plas­mid con­den­sa­tion, up­take, and ex­pres­sion,15,16 and a sep­a­rate com­par­i­son of five CPPs de­liv­er­ing GFP found pre­dom­i­nantly en­do­so­mal lo­cal­iza­tion with up­take that was both se­quence-de­pen­dent and cell-de­pen­dent.14

Mem­brane in­ter­ac­tion is real, but re­li­able cy­toso­lic or nu­clear de­liv­ery of in­tact cargo is not solved at the class level. A uni­ver­sal 1 to 4 per­cent es­cape fig­ure is not de­fen­si­ble. Func­tional de­liv­ery must be demon­strated for each con­struct with cargo-spe­cific as­says and con­trols for mem­brane dis­rup­tion, en­do­so­mal re­lease, nu­clear ac­cess, tox­i­c­ity, and in­tact tar­get en­gage­ment. The fur­thest a pep­tide-con­ju­gated oligonu­cleotide ad­vanced clin­i­cally un­der­scores the gap: SRP-5051, a pep­tide-con­ju­gated PMO dosed at 20 or 30 mg/kg in­tra­venously every four weeks in the MOMENTUM study,24 was dis­con­tin­ued,25 so its human ex­po­sure fig­ures are his­tor­i­cal ori­en­ta­tion rather than a rec­om­mended range. A CPP sys­tem with prospec­tively re­pro­ducible func­tional de­liv­ery across cell types and car­gos at a de­fined, tol­er­ated ex­po­sure would change the as­sess­ment.

Transgenic and Humanized…

Transgenic and Humanized Animal Models for Gene Therapy Validation

Trans­genic, knock-in, xenograft, and human-cell chimeric an­i­mals each an­swer dif­fer­ent pre­clin­i­cal ques­tions, and no model earns whole-sys­tem human pre­dic­tive­ness merely be­cause it con­tains a human gene, re­cep­tor, tis­sue, or im­mune com­part­ment. Model se­lec­tion should be jus­ti­fied against the prod­uct, route, tar­get bi­ol­ogy, phar­ma­col­ogy, biodis­tri­b­u­tion, and safety ques­tion, and the gov­ern­ing prin­ci­ple is to ac­cept a model only for the end­point it ac­tu­ally re­pro­duces.

  1. Func­tional vs Nom­i­nal Hu­man­iza­tion Dis­tinc­tion

In­sert­ing a human se­quence can make a tar­get-bind­ing or se­quence-edit­ing ex­per­i­ment pos­si­ble with­out recre­at­ing human reg­u­la­tion, tis­sue ar­chi­tec­ture, im­mu­nity, me­tab­o­lism, or dis­ease course, so a model is func­tion­ally rel­e­vant only for the end­point it re­pro­duces. The human trans­fer­rin re­cep­tor 1 knock-in model used with the BCY17901 con­ju­gate sup­ports re­cep­tor-de­pen­dent oligonu­cleotide de­liv­ery in the tested skele­tal and car­diac mus­cle set­ting, but that ev­i­dence is pre­clin­i­cal and spe­cific to the model; it does not in­de­pen­dently val­i­date human dose, dura­bil­ity, safety, or clin­i­cal ben­e­fit.4

Species-Specific Biology Limitations

Species dif­fer­ences can af­fect re­cep­tor bind­ing, vec­tor entry, tis­sue tro­pism, im­mune re­sponse, trans­gene ex­pres­sion, genome-edit­ing speci­ficity, and tox­i­col­ogy, and these dif­fer­ences are prod­uct-spe­cific rather than re­ducible to one cross-species cor­rec­tion fac­tor. Rel­e­vant in vitro human sys­tems, a phar­ma­co­log­i­cally re­spon­sive an­i­mal species, dis­ease mod­els, and biodis­tri­b­u­tion or tox­i­col­ogy stud­ies pro­vide com­ple­men­tary ev­i­dence rather than a sin­gle val­i­dat­ing re­sult.

  1. Human TfR1 Knock-In Mice for Pep­tide-Con­ju­gate Val­i­da­tion

Human trans­fer­rin re­cep­tor 1 knock-in mice en­abled test­ing of human-re­cep­tor bind­ing and mus­cle de­liv­ery for BCY17901-con­ju­gated ASO and siRNA con­structs.4 Human-liver chimeric TIRFA mice serve a dif­fer­ent func­tion, where murine AAV-re­cep­tor ab­la­tion re­duces com­pet­ing murine trans­duc­tion while en­grafted human he­pa­to­cytes per­mit study of AAV trans­duc­tion in the tested liver set­ting.5 Donor vari­abil­ity, en­graft­ment, im­mun­od­e­fi­ciency, and in­com­plete organ-sys­tem hu­man­iza­tion re­main lim­i­ta­tions, and the ev­i­dence stays pre­clin­i­cal and model-spe­cific.

Regulatory Use and Limitations

FDA, EMA, and ICH guid­ance re­quires a jus­ti­fied, prod­uct-spe­cific non­clin­i­cal pro­gram.1–3 An­i­mal stud­ies can sup­port proof of con­cept, phar­ma­col­ogy, biodis­tri­b­u­tion, dose-range se­lec­tion, and safety, but rel­e­vance must be ex­plained and lim­i­ta­tions car­ried for­ward. Hu­man­ized-liver stud­ies fur­ther show that donor and ex­per­i­men­tal vari­ables can ma­te­ri­ally change AAV trans­duc­tion, so no model should be de­scribed as a val­i­dated sur­ro­gate for human ef­fi­cacy with­out di­rect ev­i­dence for that use.6 The pa­ra­me­ters that mat­ter are the prod­uct-spe­cific non­clin­i­cal end­points each pro­gram must jus­tify. Prospec­tive ev­i­dence that a model's read­out pre­dicted the cor­re­spond­ing human out­come, which re­mains the ex­cep­tion rather than the rule, is the stan­dard re­quired to es­tab­lish greater stand­ing.

Computational Simulation

Computational Simulation and Personalized Prediction Frameworks

Com­pu­ta­tional tools earn their place in a gene-ther­apy pro­gram by gen­er­at­ing hy­pothe­ses, guid­ing con­struct de­sign, and plan­ning stud­ies faster and more cheaply than wet-lab it­er­a­tion alone. A model out­put is a de­sign input, not a val­i­dated pre­dic­tion of an in­di­vid­ual clin­i­cal out­come, until its per­for­mance has been demon­strated prospec­tively for that use. Each tool below has a sep­a­rate input do­main, end­point, and val­i­da­tion his­tory, and com­bin­ing their out­puts does not cre­ate clin­i­cal va­lid­ity.

  1. AAV Phys­i­o­log­i­cally-Based Phar­ma­co­ki­netic (PBPK) Biodis­tri­b­u­tion

An ex­per­i­men­tal mouse AAV8 and AAV9 PBPK model linked vec­tor dis­tri­b­u­tion, tis­sue pro­cess­ing, trans­gene ex­pres­sion, and dis­po­si­tion of a se­creted trans­gene prod­uct, cap­tur­ing the study data and pro­vid­ing a quan­ti­ta­tive foun­da­tion for pre­clin­i­cal de­sign and trans­la­tional re­search.7 Or­thog­o­nal imag­ing sup­ports the same biodis­tri­b­u­tion ques­tion from a dif­fer­ent angle, as in PET as­sess­ment of la­beled AAV cap­sid dis­tri­b­u­tion.8 Nei­ther is a val­i­dated pa­tient-spe­cific model, and re­sults should not be gen­er­al­ized to other cap­sids, car­gos, routes, species, or clin­i­cal pop­u­la­tions with­out ex­ter­nal qual­i­fi­ca­tion.

  1. mRNA Codon Op­ti­miza­tion Al­go­rithms

LinearDesign jointly op­ti­mizes cod­ing se­quence and mod­eled mRNA sec­ondary-struc­ture sta­bil­ity, and the study re­ported im­proved sta­bil­ity and ex­pres­sion in tested ex­per­i­men­tal sys­tems, in­clud­ing vac­cine con­structs.9 Be­cause codon choice also al­ters RNA struc­ture, trans­la­tion ki­net­ics, in­nate sens­ing, and pro­tein fold­ing, al­go­rith­mic scores are de­sign in­puts rather than proof of clin­i­cal su­pe­ri­or­ity, and the pub­lished ev­i­dence does not sup­port in­di­vid­u­al­ized op­ti­miza­tion from a pa­tient's tRNA pro­file.

  1. AlphaFold2 Struc­tural Mod­el­ing for Pep­tides and Tar­gets

AlphaFold2 es­tab­lished high ac­cu­racy for many pro­tein struc­tures in its bench­mark set­ting,10 but a sep­a­rate bench­mark of 588 ex­per­i­men­tally char­ac­ter­ized pep­tides found vari­able pep­tide per­for­mance and doc­u­mented in­ac­cu­rate cases.11 Con­fi­dence scores, miss­ing lig­ands or mod­i­fi­ca­tions, con­for­ma­tional dy­nam­ics, and the dif­fer­ence be­tween a monomer struc­ture and a bind­ing pre­dic­tion all have to stay vis­i­ble, be­cause a pre­dicted fold or in­ter­face is not proof of affin­ity, func­tion, speci­ficity, or safety. It is a val­i­dated struc­ture-pre­dic­tion method whose ap­plic­a­bil­ity to pep­tides is bounded.

  1. In­te­gra­tion and Clin­i­cal Trans­la­tion Bot­tle­necks

Each tool has a sep­a­rate input do­main, end­point, un­cer­tainty model, and val­i­da­tion his­tory, so com­bin­ing out­puts does not cre­ate clin­i­cal va­lid­ity. A work­flow in­tended for clin­i­cal use should state the model ver­sion, train­ing or cal­i­bra­tion do­main, input prove­nance, ap­plic­a­bil­ity lim­its, sen­si­tiv­ity analy­sis, and prospec­tive or ex­ter­nal val­i­da­tion sta­tus.12 No time-to-clin­i­cal-avail­abil­ity fore­cast is jus­ti­fied here. The pa­ra­me­ters that mat­ter are each model's stated do­main of va­lid­ity and mea­sured pre­dic­tive per­for­mance. Prospec­tive, ex­ter­nally val­i­dated ev­i­dence that a tool's out­put im­proved a real clin­i­cal de­ci­sion is needed to change the cur­rent as­sess­ment.

Cellular Endotoxicology

Cellular Endotoxicology in Gene Therapy Manufacturing

En­do­toxin is both a process im­pu­rity and a po­tent bi­o­log­i­cal stim­u­lus in gene-ther­apy man­u­fac­tur­ing, but those roles do not es­tab­lish a uni­ver­sal thresh­old. For an in­ves­ti­ga­tional prod­uct, the source ma­te­ri­als, man­u­fac­tur­ing process, route, max­i­mum ad­min­is­tered dose, ma­trix ef­fects, an­a­lyt­i­cal method, and ac­cep­tance cri­te­ria de­ter­mine the spec­i­fi­ca­tion. FDA guid­ance calls for phase-ap­pro­pri­ate char­ac­ter­i­za­tion and con­trol of en­do­toxin, py­ro­genic­ity, and other process-re­lated im­pu­ri­ties; it does not es­tab­lish a class-wide cel­lu­lar low­est-ob­served-ad­verse-ef­fect level.13,14

  1. Plas­mid En­do­toxin Co-Pu­rifi­ca­tion in Gene Ther­apy

Bac­te­ri­ally pro­duced plas­mid DNA can carry lipopolysac­cha­ride into a trans­fec­tion or vec­tor-pro­duc­tion work­flow, and the ef­fects are not uni­form across cells or processes. In one multi-cell-line study, rel­a­tively high en­do­toxin loads were re­quired to re­duce pro­lif­er­a­tion or trans­fec­tion in most tested lines.15 In a sep­a­rate rAAV study, plas­mids from a mod­i­fied low-en­do­tox­i­c­ity Es­cherichia coli strain yielded vec­tor prepa­ra­tions as­so­ci­ated with less NF-kB sig­nal­ing in the tested cell and mouse-retina sys­tems.16 These re­sults sup­port im­pu­rity con­trol and assay val­i­da­tion through a prod­uct-bound spec­i­fi­ca­tion, not a trans­fer­able safe or toxic cut­off or class-wide work­ing range.

  1. Cas­pase-4/5 Py­rop­to­sis Path­way Ac­ti­va­tion

Human cas­pase-4 and cas­pase-5 can bind cy­toso­lic lipopolysac­cha­ride, and cas­pase-4 is a major dri­ver of py­rop­totic death in sev­eral tested human cell sys­tems.17 Down­stream IL-1beta mat­u­ra­tion can in­volve NLRP3 and cas­pase-1, with the rel­a­tive con­tri­bu­tion of cas­pase-5 vary­ing by stim­u­lus and model.18 These mech­a­nis­tic ex­per­i­ments used in­tra­cel­lu­lar de­liv­ery or in­fec­tion, so they do not trans­late di­rectly into an ex­tra­cel­lu­lar en­do­toxin spec­i­fi­ca­tion for a man­u­fac­tured cell or gene-ther­apy prod­uct.

  1. LPS LOAEL and Thresh­old Ef­fects

A com­mon and con­se­quen­tial error is im­port­ing a low­est-ob­served-ad­verse-ef­fect level from the lit­er­a­ture as a prod­uct spec­i­fi­ca­tion. No sin­gle LPS LOAEL trans­fers across cell types, ex­po­sure routes, assay ma­tri­ces, and prod­ucts; FDA guid­ance calls for a phase-ap­pro­pri­ate, prod­uct-spe­cific en­do­toxin spec­i­fi­ca­tion rather than a class-wide cel­lu­lar thresh­old.13,14 The spe­cific rea­son a bor­rowed num­ber mis­leads is a cat­e­gory error. Trans­fec­tion stud­ies, in which rel­a­tively high en­do­toxin loads were needed be­fore pro­lif­er­a­tion or trans­fec­tion changed,15 and in­tra­cel­lu­lar-LPS stud­ies, in which cy­toso­lic lipopolysac­cha­ride dri­ves cas­pase-4 py­rop­totic death,17 mea­sure dif­fer­ent ex­po­sure con­di­tions and end­points, so each an­swers a dif­fer­ent ques­tion than the one a spec­i­fi­ca­tion must an­swer. A de­fen­si­ble limit is jus­ti­fied against the in­tended dose and route, method suit­abil­ity, process ca­pa­bil­ity, and the com­plete con­trol strat­egy.

  1. En­do­toxin Tol­er­ance and Man­u­fac­tur­ing Timescales

Re­peated ex­po­sure can alter in­nate-cell re­spon­sive­ness in ex­per­i­men­tal sys­tems, but that bi­o­log­i­cal phe­nom­e­non is not a man­u­fac­tur­ing al­lowance. Ap­par­ent tol­er­ance can also mask cell-state changes rel­e­vant to iden­tity, po­tency, vi­a­bil­ity, or in­flam­ma­tory func­tion, so a man­u­fac­tur­ing process should con­trol the im­pu­rity and eval­u­ate prod­uct-spe­cific con­se­quences rather than re­ly­ing on adap­ta­tion, a fixed ex­po­sure du­ra­tion, or a pre­sumed re­cov­ery pe­riod. Across this frame­work the gov­ern­ing num­bers are prod­uct-spe­cific en­do­toxin spec­i­fi­ca­tions set against dose, route, and method, not a uni­ver­sal cel­lu­lar thresh­old; method-qual­i­fied, prod­uct-spe­cific data link­ing a de­fined en­do­toxin level to a mea­sured change in that prod­uct's iden­tity or po­tency would sharpen the con­trol basis for any given prod­uct.
