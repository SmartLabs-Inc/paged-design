Chapter 04

Mitochondrial Decline

Why cells stop making enough energy, and what can be done about it

Of the recognized hallmarks of aging, mitochondrial dysfunction is the one patients feel first and clinicians measure last. This chapter takes it in the order the patient experiences it.

Learning objectives

  1. Describe the relationship between mitochondrial density and functional capacity across the adult lifespan.
  2. Distinguish primary mitochondrial disease from the acquired decline seen in ordinary aging.
  3. Interpret a lactate-to-pyruvate ratio in the context of a full metabolic panel.
  4. Select an intervention appropriate to the evidence tier, and say honestly which tier it sits in.

4.1 What actually declines

A muscle biopsy from a healthy person of seventy contains roughly half the mitochondrial DNA copy number of one taken at twenty-five. That much is uncontroversial. What the number conceals is that the loss is not evenly distributed: it concentrates in the tissues with the highest resting demand, which is why the heart, the brain, and the proximal skeletal muscle groups declare themselves before anything else does.

A note on copy number

Copy number is not the same as function. A cell can carry a normal complement of mitochondrial DNA and still generate very little usable energy if the electron transport chain is uncoupled.

The clinical consequence is that a patient will describe stairs before they describe anything a standard panel would catch. They rarely use the word fatigue. They say they have started taking the elevator, or that they no longer carry the shopping in one trip. These are functional statements, and they precede the biochemical ones by years.

This matters for how the history is taken. A question about energy invites a subjective answer that is almost impossible to score. A question about a specific task — how many flights before you stop — produces something that can be repeated at the next visit and compared.

In practice

Ask what the patient has stopped doing rather than how they feel. The first is a measurement; the second is a mood.

4.2 Mechanism

Three processes run together, and separating them is what makes the difference between treating a mechanism and treating a symptom.

Stage One

Accumulation

Somatic mutations build up in mitochondrial DNA faster than in nuclear DNA, with no comparable repair pathway.

Stage Two

Uncoupling

Proton leak across the inner membrane rises, so more substrate is consumed for the same output.

Stage Three

Attrition

Damaged organelles are cleared faster than they are replaced, and total density falls.

Stage Four

Compensation

Glycolytic flux rises to cover the shortfall, which is why lactate is the first thing to move.

Only the fourth stage is visible on an ordinary panel, and by the time it is visible the first three have been running for a decade or more. That lag is the single most important fact in this chapter.

The clinical point

By the time the laboratory agrees with the patient, the patient has been right for ten years.

4.3 Reading the curve

Figure 4.1

Mitochondrial DNA copy number in vastus lateralis against age, pooled from four cross-sectional cohorts. The shaded band is the interquartile range, not a confidence interval.

Source: pooled cohort data, reproduced for illustration.

Note the width of the band rather than the slope of the line. At any given age the spread between the first and third quartile is wider than the entire mean decline across two decades. Population curves describe populations; they do not describe the person in front of you, and a patient at seventy in the top quartile is in better shape than one at fifty in the bottom.

What the curve shows

A real, reproducible decline in mean copy number with age across every cohort measured.

What it does not

Any basis for predicting an individual, or for treating a number rather than a symptom.

Chapter 05

Assessment and Intervention

What to measure, what to offer, and how strong the evidence actually is

Every intervention below is classified by evidence tier. The tier is part of the recommendation, not a footnote to it.

5.1 Evidence tiers

This textbook uses four tiers throughout. They are stated plainly because a reader deciding what to offer a patient deserves to know how firm the ground is.

Established

Replicated in adequately powered randomized trials with clinical endpoints.

Supported

Consistent trial evidence, but on surrogate endpoints or in narrow populations.

Emerging

Mechanistically coherent with early human data. Reasonable to discuss, premature to promise.

Experimental

Animal or in-vitro only. Belongs in a conversation about research, not a treatment plan.

Before you order the panel

A raised lactate in an untrained patient who walked up two flights to the clinic is a measurement of the stairs, not of the mitochondria. Rest them fifteen minutes first.

The panel is cheap, widely available, and almost never ordered together — which is the reason it is set out here as a panel rather than as six separate tests. The ratio carries information that neither value carries alone, and it can only be computed if both are drawn at the same time.

5.3 What to offer

Four interventions have enough behind them to be offered routinely. They are given in descending order of evidence, which is also, inconveniently, ascending order of patient enthusiasm.

One

Progressive resistance training

The only intervention that reliably raises mitochondrial density in human skeletal muscle. Effect sizes exceed anything pharmacological by a wide margin, and it is Established.

Two

Zone-two aerobic work

Improves oxidative capacity and substrate flexibility. Established for capacity, Supported for clinical endpoints in older adults.

Three

Sleep consolidation

Mitophagy is sleep-dependent. Fragmented sleep impairs clearance of damaged organelles. Supported.

Four

NAD+ precursor supplementation

Raises tissue NAD+ reliably. Whether that translates to a clinical endpoint is unsettled. Emerging, and it should be described that way to the patient.1

Say the tier out loud

A patient told that a supplement is emerging and a patient told that it works are two different patients, and only one of them has given informed consent.

5.4 A twelve-week protocol

What follows is a worked example, not a prescription. Boxes mark what the patient does; dashes mark what the clinic does.

Weeks 01 – 04

Baseline and loading

The patient

  • Two resistance sessions a week, supervised for the first four.
  • Record flights of stairs before stopping, once a week, same staircase.

The clinic

  • Draw the full panel at rest.
  • Establish a one-repetition maximum for three compound lifts.

Weeks 05 – 12

Progression and review

The patient

  • Three sessions a week, load increased when the last set is completed cleanly.
  • Add two zone-two sessions of forty minutes.

The clinic

  • Repeat the panel at week twelve, rested, same time of day.
  • Compare stairs against the week-one figure before comparing any laboratory value.

Chapter summary

  1. Functional decline precedes biochemical evidence by years. Take the history accordingly.
  2. The lactate-to-pyruvate ratio carries information neither value carries alone.
  3. Resistance training outperforms every pharmacological option available today.
  4. State the evidence tier when you make the recommendation, not afterwards.
