5Alpha-Synuclein Prion-Like Propagation

Alpha-Synuclein Prion-Like Propagation

Tem­plated alpha-synu­clein mis­fold­ing links exper­i­men­tal prop­a­ga­tion models with seed-ampli­fi­ca­tion assays (SAAs) that detect dis­ease-asso­ci­ated activ­ity in

human biospec­i­mens. Mech­a­nis­tic prop­a­ga­tion, diag­nos­tic clas­si­fi­ca­tion and prog­nos­tic use remain dif­fer­ent evi­dence ques­tions.3879,3880,3883,3884

Research Uses

Prop­a­ga­tion And Tox­i­c­ity: Pri­mary-neuron work con­nected recruit­ment of endoge­nous alpha-synu­clein into Lewy-like inclu­sions with synap­tic dys­func­tion

and neu­ronal death. Mouse exper­i­ments linked prop­a­ga­tion with nigros­tri­atal loss and motor abnor­mal­i­ties, while patient-derived Lewy-body mate­r­ial induced related pathol­ogy in mice and non­hu­man pri­mates. These models sup­port host-pro­tein recruit­ment and prop­a­ga­tion as mech­a­nis­tic tar­gets under defined exper­i­men­tal con­di­tions.3879–3882

Dis­ease-Asso­ci­ated Seed­ing: Human CSF stud­ies detected patho­log­i­cal seed­ing and dis­tin­guished aggre­gate sig­na­tures asso­ci­ated with Parkin­son dis­ease and mul­ti­ple system atro­phy. In this con­text, prion-like describes tem­plated mis­fold­ing and exper­i­men­tal prop­a­ga­tion; it does not mean that these dis­or­ders are shown to spread between people.3883,3884

Pro­to­col Depen­dence: In a 60-par­tic­i­pant CSF com­par­i­son, two seed-ampli­fi­ca­tion pro­to­cols agreed poorly in clin­i­cally diag­nosed mul­ti­ple system atro­phy despite high agree­ment between lab­o­ra­to­ries using the same pro­to­col. Among the 31 non-MSA sam­ples, 16 Parkin­son dis­ease or demen­tia with Lewy bodies com­par­i­son spec­i­mens had been screened pre­vi­ously under the phos­phate-buffered con­di­tions, lim­it­ing inter­pre­ta­tion of that subset as an unbi­ased clin­i­cal sen­si­tiv­ity sample. A PIPES-buffered pro­to­col detected type-2 seeds in 19 and 21 of 29 MSA sam­ples at its two sites, whereas a phos­phate-buffered pro­to­col detected no type-2 seeds; its two pos­i­tive MSA clas­si­fi­ca­tions showed a type-1 pat­tern. The modest cohort, clin­i­cal ref­er­ence diag­no­sis and exclu­sion of incon­clu­sive results from sen­si­tiv­ity cal­cu­la­tions limit gen­er­al­iza­tion.4017

CSF Sub­group­ing: A 2023 PPMI cross-sec­tional study of 1,123 par­tic­i­pants reported 87.7% sen­si­tiv­ity for Parkin­son dis­ease and 96.3% speci­ficity against healthy con­trols. Pos­i­tiv­ity dif­fered by geno­type and olfac­tory status, includ­ing lower pos­i­tiv­ity in LRRK2-asso­ci­ated dis­ease and pre­served olfac­tion. Pos­i­tive pro­dro­mal results sup­port bio­marker-defined recruit­ment, while het­ero­gene­ity limits inter­pre­ta­tion of a neg­a­tive assay.3836

Skin Work­flow And Han­dling: A skin-spe­cific SAA in 332 clin­i­cally diag­nosed Parkin­son dis­ease cases and 285 con­trols reported 92.46% sen­si­tiv­ity, 93.33% speci­ficity and inter­lab­o­ra­tory kappa 0.87. Seeds were stable at minus 80 degrees Cel­sius but vul­ner­a­ble to even short warmer stor­age and exces­sive grind­ing. These fig­ures belong to the spec­i­fied cer­vi­cal-skin work­flow and clin­i­cally clas­si­fied cohort, with pre­pro­cess­ing and site con­di­tions treated as per­for­mance vari­ables.3838

Matched-Matrix Per­for­mance: Uni­form-pro­to­col test­ing in prob­a­ble demen­tia with Lewy bodies showed strong matrix depen­dence. Among CSF-pos­i­tive cases, skin sen­si­tiv­ity and speci­ficity were 94% and 98%, olfac­tory mucosa 47% and 100%, urine 22% and 100%, and saliva seed­ing was unde­tectable. The single-center, unblinded, cross-sec­tional design and con­di­tion­ing non-CSF sen­si­tiv­ity on CSF pos­i­tiv­ity limit broader diag­nos­tic com­par­isons.3842

Ear­li­est-Stage Qual­i­fi­ca­tion: In a pre­lim­i­nary 2026 autopsy-con­firmed series, CSF SAA was more sen­si­tive than skin or sub­mandibu­lar-gland test­ing in inci­den­tal Lewy body dis­ease, whereas all three tis­sues per­formed better in estab­lished Parkin­son dis­ease. Small and incom­plete ret­ro­spec­tive tissue sub­sets and abstract-only access con­strain the result, but it directly cau­tions against extend­ing periph­eral-tissue per­for­mance to the ear­li­est patho­log­i­cal stage.3851

Pro­gres­sion Research: In pro­dro­mal par­tic­i­pants, pos­i­tive seed­ing and faster ampli­fi­ca­tion were asso­ci­ated with higher phe­no­con­ver­sion risk, while repeat tests in a smaller sub­group remained unchanged. In man­i­fest Parkin­son dis­ease, base­line assay para­me­ters did not show clin­i­cally mean­ing­ful asso­ci­a­tions with motor, non-motor, cog­ni­tive or imag­ing pro­gres­sion. Risk strat­i­fi­ca­tion before diag­no­sis and track­ing decline after­ward are there­fore sep­a­rate pro­posed uses.3841,3845

Interpretive Considerations

Assay Mean­ing: Biospec­i­men matrix, reac­tion con­di­tions, col­lec­tion site, tissue stage, pro­cess­ing, stor­age, geno­type, olfac­tion and cohort def­i­n­i­tion can change SAA

inter­pre­ta­tion4017. Cross-sec­tional sen­si­tiv­ity is not an indi­vid­ual fore­cast of con­ver­sion time, and prop­a­ga­tion in an exper­i­men­tal model does not estab­lish that an inter­ven­tion will improve human dis­ease.3836,3838,3842,3851

6Native GIP and GIP-Receptor Pharmacology

Native GIP and GIP-Receptor Pharmacology

Native glu­cose-depen­dent insulinotropic polypep­tide (GIP) is a 42-amino-acid hor­mone released by pro­te­olytic pro­cess­ing of a 153-residue pre­cur­sor,

pre­proGIP.1543 It is secreted by small-intesti­nal K cells in response to nutri­ent intake.3829 Native GIP, recep­tor-antag­o­nist frag­ments and engi­neered multi-recep­tor ago­nists answer dif­fer­ent ques­tions. Glu­cose state, dis­ease state, tissue, recep­tor phar­ma­col­ogy and expo­sure deter­mine whether a result reflects endocrine reg­u­la­tion, vas­cu­lar phys­i­ol­ogy or an engi­neered con­struct.3829,3885,1713

See the metabolic-agent comparison for selected agents by con­struct and recep­tor activ­ity.

Applications

Glu­cose-Depen­dent Endocrine Reg­u­la­tion: In ten healthy men, GIP increased insulin secre­tion during hyper­glycemia with­out increas­ing glucagon, while at

fast­ing glu­cose and during hypo­glycemia it increased glucagon with little or no insulin effect3829. In an acute ran­dom­ized crossover study of four men and six women with type 2 dia­betes during main­tained hyper­glycemia, phar­ma­co­log­i­cal intra­venous GIP increased insulin and C-pep­tide responses but atten­u­ated glucagon sup­pres­sion rel­a­tive to saline3853. The same expo­sure increased heart rate, low­ered sys­tolic and dias­tolic blood pres­sure, raised glu­cose-infu­sion require­ments, and reduced the M/I ratio, a sur­ro­gate of insulin sen­si­tiv­ity. These acute sys­temic find­ings do not estab­lish chronic car­dio­vas­cu­lar or meta­bolic ben­e­fit or harm3853. In C-pep­tide-neg­a­tive type 1 dia­betes, GIP did not restore glucagon coun­ter­reg­u­la­tion during insulin-induced hypo­glycemia.3848

Sus­tained Native-GIP Expo­sure: A 2026 ran­dom­ized trial assigned 61 people with type 2 dia­betes to six weeks of con­tin­u­ous sub­cu­ta­neous native GIP or placebo, alone or added to semaglu­tide. Nei­ther pre­spec­i­fied com­par­i­son sig­nif­i­cantly improved mean sensor glu­cose; 10 par­tic­i­pants dis­con­tin­ued and 22 had injec­tion-site reac­tions. Total cir­cu­lat­ing GIP rose much more than intact bioac­tive GIP, so deliv­ery, degra­da­tion and expo­sure form matter. The single-center, all-White cohort and dropout pat­tern limit firm con­clu­sions, espe­cially for add-on treat­ment.3849

Adi­pose Per­fu­sion: During a hyper­glycemic-hyper­in­su­line­mic clamp in eight lean vol­un­teers, GIP increased abdom­i­nal adi­pose blood flow and microvas­cu­lar blood volume. An ear­lier six-person clamp study found that insulin was per­mis­sive for GIP-asso­ci­ated sub­cu­ta­neous adi­pose blood flow and triglyc­eride clear­ance, whereas C-pep­tide was not required. These acute results sup­port cap­il­lary recruit­ment and nutri­ent-han­dling research, not chronic weight-effect claims.3832,3831

Splanch­nic Per­fu­sion: In healthy men, GIP increased supe­rior mesen­teric artery blood flow and recep­tor antag­o­nism reduced the post-glu­cose increase with­out sim­i­lar hepatic or celiac changes. A 2026 crossover study in ten people with type 2 dia­betes like­wise found an endoge­nous GIP con­tri­bu­tion to oral-glu­cose-induced supe­rior mesen­teric flow; antag­o­nism low­ered insulin and C-pep­tide with­out chang­ing glu­cose or glucagon. These small acute stud­ies sup­port a regional post­pran­dial vas­cu­lar role.3843,3852

Skele­tal Turnover: GIP acutely sup­pressed the bone-resorp­tion marker CTX in twelve men with type 2 dia­betes, with­out cor­re­spond­ing 90-minute dif­fer­ences in P1NP or PTH. In twelve totally pan­cre­a­te­c­tomized par­tic­i­pants, GIPR antag­o­nism atten­u­ated meal-induced sup­pres­sion of bone resorp­tion but did not mea­sur­ably change glu­cose, gas­tric emp­ty­ing, appetite, intake, triglyc­erides or hemo­dy­nam­ics. The latter sup­ports a pan­creas-inde­pen­dent bone signal while con­strain­ing claims of broad extra­pan­cre­atic effects.3833,3846

Human Recep­tor Probe: GIP(3-30)NH2 reduced GIP-induced insulin secre­tion by 82% during a human hyper­glycemic clamp. Recep­tor exper­i­ments found inhi­bi­tion of G-pro­tein and arrestin-linked sig­nal­ing, high affin­ity at human and non­hu­man-pri­mate GIPR and much lower affin­ity at rodent GIPR. This makes the frag­ment useful for human target attri­bu­tion while pre­serv­ing species limits.3885,1556

Engi­neered Ago­nists And Cir­cuits: Tirzepatide showed GIP-like GIPR activ­ity but GLP-1R sig­nal­ing biased toward cAMP with less arrestin recruit­ment and inter­nal­iza­tion than GLP-1. In mice, area-postrema GIP recep­tors con­tributed to appetite sup­pres­sion by acyl-GIP, whereas hypo­thal­a­mic recep­tors medi­ated addi­tional weight loss from GIPR antag­o­nism com­bined with liraglu­tide. These con­struct- and region-spe­cific data offer a cir­cuit-level account of appar­ently oppos­ing strate­gies with­out making native GIP inter­change­able with either con­struct.1713,3886,3854

Practical Considerations

Expo­sure And Con­text: The human antag­o­nist clamp used GIP(3-30)NH2 at 800 pmol/kg/min and GIP at 1.5 pmol/kg/min; those con­trolled infu­sion rates do not define gen­eral treat­ment

doses. Native hor­mone, antag­o­nist frag­ment, acy­lated ago­nist and dual ago­nist require sep­a­rate attri­bu­tion. Glu­cose and insulin state, acute versus six-week expo­sure, sur­gi­cal anatomy, pop­u­la­tion, species and the mea­sured end­point should remain beside each inter­pre­ta­tion.3885,1556,3849,3846

7GLP-1 Active Metabolite and Chimeric Advances

GLP-1 Active Metabolite and Chimeric Advances

The endoge­nous GLP-1(9-36) metabo­lite, pep­tide-cargo con­ju­gates, anti­body-pep­tide con­ju­gates, multi-recep­tor pep­tides and nucleic-acid deliv­ery plat­forms are

dis­tinct con­structs. Their mech­a­nisms depend on the active moiety, link­age, recep­tor bal­ance, tissue tar­get­ing and dura­tion of expo­sure.3887,1560,3891,3837

The metabolic-agent comparison dis­tin­guishes selected single-mol­e­cule ago­nists from a two-agent com­bi­na­tion; the metabo­lites and engi­neered plat­forms here should retain their exact iden­ti­ties.3887,3837

Applications

Metabo­lite Phys­i­ol­ogy: GLP-1(9-36) amide is gen­er­ated by DPP-4 and sup­pressed glucagon in mouse and human islets. Activ­ity per­sisted after clas­si­cal

GLP-1R inac­ti­va­tion and involved inhibitory G-pro­tein sig­nal­ing, reduced cal­cium entry and fewer docked gran­ules; glucagon-recep­tor antag­o­nism blocked key effects. The response was absent in alpha cells from donors with type 2 dia­betes, and a mouse hypo­glycemia effect depended on pre­treat­ment timing. Ear­lier healthy-human infu­sion did not mea­sur­ably improve glu­cose tol­er­ance or insulin secre­tion.3887,3888

Vas­cu­lar And Car­diac Func­tion: Mouse bind­ing and aortic-response exper­i­ments sug­gested GLP-1(9-36) activ­ity through a path­way dis­tinct from clas­si­cal GLP-1R. After myocar­dial infarc­tion in mice, the metabo­lite pre­served selected dias­tolic mea­sures and reduced macrophage infil­tra­tion and inflam­ma­tory cytokine expres­sion, while infarct size, sys­tolic dys­func­tion and fibro­sis were unchanged. The pre­clin­i­cal prospect is selec­tive remod­el­ing or inflam­ma­tory mod­u­la­tion.3889,3890

Tar­geted Neu­ronal Deliv­ery: A cleav­ably linked GLP-1-MK-801 con­struct paired GLP-1R ago­nism with tar­geted NMDA-recep­tor antag­o­nism in rodents. It improved obe­sity-related meta­bolic out­comes and altered hypo­thal­a­mic and brain­stem sig­nal­ing while avoid­ing selected adverse effects of untar­geted MK-801 in those exper­i­ments. The result belongs to the linked mol­e­cule rather than coad­min­is­tra­tion of its sep­a­rate com­po­nents.1560

Multi-Recep­tor Tissue Effects: GEP44 and GEP12 target GLP-1, neu­ropep­tide Y1 and neu­ropep­tide Y2 recep­tors. Y1 sig­nal­ing opposed part of the GLP-1-linked insulin response in rat and human islets, while ex vivo muscle showed Y1-medi­ated, insulin-inde­pen­dent glu­cose uptake. In obese rats, the pep­tides reduced food intake and weight more than liraglu­tide in the cited study. Tissue-spe­cific recep­tor inter­ac­tions there­fore matter more than the label triple ago­nist.3891

Anti­body-Pep­tide Con­ju­gate: AMG 133, or maride­bart cafraglu­tide, com­bines a fully human anti-GIPR antag­o­nist anti­body with two GLP-1 ana­logue ago­nist pep­tides. A spon­sor-linked ran­dom­ized phase 1 study included 49 single-ascend­ing-dose and 26 mul­ti­ple-ascend­ing-dose par­tic­i­pants with obe­sity; mul­ti­ple doses were sub­cu­ta­neous every four weeks. Safety and tol­er­a­bil­ity were pri­mary, weight was exploratory, gas­troin­testi­nal events were common, and the high­est mul­ti­ple-dose cohort did not com­plete all planned dosing.3837

Phase 2 Human Evi­dence: A sub­se­quent ran­dom­ized, placebo-con­trolled dose-rang­ing trial enrolled 592 par­tic­i­pants, 465 with obe­sity and 127 with obe­sity and type 2 dia­betes. It tested sev­eral four-week sched­ules and one eight-week sched­ule. At week 52, treat­ment-policy mean weight changes across active groups ranged from -12.3% to -16.2% versus -2.5% with placebo in the obe­sity cohort, and from -8.4% to -12.3% versus -1.7% in the obe­sity-dia­betes cohort. In the latter cohort, gly­cated hemo­glo­bin fell by 1.2 to 1.6 per­cent­age points with active treat­ment versus a 0.1-point rise with placebo. Gas­troin­testi­nal adverse events were common but less fre­quent with dose esca­la­tion and lower start­ing doses; the authors reported no unex­pected safety sig­nals. The ranges com­bine dose and sched­ule groups and do not estab­lish a single opti­mal reg­i­men.4004

Con­struct Opti­miza­tion: Spon­sor-asso­ci­ated dis­cov­ery work opti­mized AMG 133 for pro­longed expo­sure of struc­turally intact GLP-1 pep­tide on an anti-GIPR scaf­fold. Potency, pep­tide sta­bil­ity, linker and con­ju­ga­tion affected per­for­mance, while recep­tor over­ex­pres­sion could com­press potency dif­fer­ences and some vari­ants were not advanced because of phar­ma­co­ki­netic vari­abil­ity or immuno­genic­ity con­cerns. Phase III and pos­si­ble monthly-dosing state­ments in that paper are spon­sor devel­op­ment-status claims, not inde­pen­dent effi­cacy results.3847

DNA Deliv­ery Plat­form: A 2026 pre­clin­i­cal study encoded Fc-fused, cleav­age-resis­tant incretin ana­logues and a dual GLP-1R/GIPR con­struct in plas­mid DNA for intra­mus­cu­lar elec­tro­po­ra­tion. One admin­is­tra­tion pro­duced sus­tained expres­sion and meta­bolic effects in diet-induced obese mice. This estab­lishes a nucleic-acid deliv­ery con­cept, not admin­is­tra­tion of native pep­tide; species, diet-switch, T-cell-deple­tion, titra­tion and long-term target-cell ques­tions remain unre­solved.3850

Practical Considerations

Con­struct And Evi­dence Level: Metabo­lite timing and donor dis­ease state, cleav­able neu­ronal tar­get­ing, recep­tor bal­ance, anti­body con­ju­ga­tion and gene-based

expres­sion each change the tested inter­ven­tion. The AMG 133 human record now includes phase 1 dose-esca­la­tion and pub­lished phase 2 dose-rang­ing results, while the DNA plat­form remains mouse evi­dence. Native GLP-1(9-36), sep­a­rate drugs, AMG 133 and expressed designer incretins should not be treated as equiv­a­lent expo­sures.3887,​1560,​3837,​4004,​3850

8Obestatin and Ghrelin Opposition Axis

Obestatin and Ghrelin Opposition Axis

Obestatin is a 23-amino-acid pep­tide encoded by the ghre­lin gene and derived from pre­proghre­lin. Shar­ing a pre­cur­sor does not create a uni­ver­sal rec­i­p­ro­cal

switch: Appetite, pan­cre­atic secre­tion, muscle repair and cir­cu­lat­ing bio­marker stud­ies pro­duce tissue- and assay-spe­cific results.708,3892,704

The obestatin entry devel­ops sur­vival, vas­cu­lar, skele­tal-muscle and tissue-repair appli­ca­tions. This entry focuses on the ghre­lin rela­tion­ship and cir­cu­lat­ing mea­sure­ments, while directly bind­ing the human-myoblast and Duchenne mus­cu­lar dys­tro­phy evi­dence that makes the muscle cross-ref­er­ence mate­r­ial.3830,712

Applications

Appetite And Recep­tor Hypothe­ses: The orig­i­nal report described obestatin as an anorec­tic GPR39 ligand, but later direct recep­tor exper­i­ments failed to show

func­tional GPR39 acti­va­tion. Inde­pen­dent feed­ing stud­ies in lean, obese and ghre­lin-knock­out rodents also failed to repro­duce appetite or gas­tric-motil­ity effects. The orig­i­nal assign­ment is there­fore his­tor­i­cal evi­dence rather than an accepted recep­tor or appetite mech­a­nism.3827,3892,3893,699

Pan­cre­atic Secre­tion: Obestatin enhanced glu­cose-stim­u­lated insulin secre­tion in insuli­noma cells, iso­lated islets and mice. Sup­press­ing or delet­ing GHS-R, includ­ing beta-cell-spe­cific dele­tion, reduced or removed the response. This sup­ports a GHS-R-depen­dent secre­tory mech­a­nism in those sys­tems and con­trasts with ghre­lin’s inhibitory action, with­out estab­lish­ing one uni­ver­sal obestatin recep­tor.704

Muscle-Regen­er­a­tion Cross-Ref­er­ence: Human myoblast work reported an autocrine or paracrine obestatin-GPR39 pro­gram involv­ing stage-depen­dent ERK1/2 and beta-arrestin scaf­fold­ing, EGFR trans­ac­ti­va­tion, cell-cycle exit, dif­fer­en­ti­a­tion and myotube hyper­tro­phy. In human dys­trophic myotubes and mdx mice, a 2025 study linked obestatin with cal­cineurin, TFEB and NFATc1 sig­nal­ing, mito­chon­dr­ial and autophagic pro­grams, utrophin and sar­colem­mal pro­teins, lower cre­a­tine kinase and improved muscle force. These cell and mouse find­ings strengthen the cur­rent E074 tissue-repair account; pro­posed monother­apy or com­bi­na­tions remain pre­clin­i­cal con­cepts.3830,712

Meta­bolic Bio­marker Research: Cir­cu­lat­ing obestatin was lower in 47 people with type 2 dia­betes and 30 with impaired glu­cose reg­u­la­tion than in 38 matched con­trols, with asso­ci­a­tions involv­ing insulin resis­tance and body-fat dis­tri­b­u­tion. This obser­va­tional evi­dence sup­ports bio­marker research but cannot deter­mine whether the change causes or fol­lows the meta­bolic state.3894

Inflam­ma­tory Bowel Dis­ease Mea­sure­ments: In 31 people with Crohn dis­ease and 22 with ulcer­a­tive col­i­tis, active dis­ease was asso­ci­ated with higher ghre­lin and a lower obestatin-to-ghre­lin ratio. Obestatin alone did not differ sig­nif­i­cantly. The ratio signal may there­fore be driven mainly by ghre­lin and remains a cohort-based activ­ity find­ing.3895

Practical Considerations

Recep­tor, Tissue And Mea­sure­ment Con­text: Direct recep­tor tests, feed­ing stud­ies, beta-cell per­tur­ba­tion, human myoblast sig­nal­ing, mdx-mouse func­tion

and cir­cu­lat­ing ratios should be inter­preted sep­a­rately. A ratio requires its numer­a­tor, denom­i­na­tor, pep­tide forms and assay con­text, while a regen­er­a­tive signal in cells or mice does not resolve the orig­i­nal appetite-recep­tor dis­pute or estab­lish a human muscle treat­ment.3892,​704,​3830,​712,​3895

9LL-37 as Multi-Modal Innate Immunity Effector

LL-37 as Multi-Modal Innate Immunity Effector

Pro­te­olytic release of LL-37 from hCAP18 pro­duces a cathe­li­cidin effec­tor with antimi­cro­bial, leuko­cyte-recruit­ing, epithe­lial-repair and inflam­ma­tory

actions. Which action dom­i­nates depends on the tissue and for­mu­la­tion

Applications

Immune Recruit­ment and Bar­rier Repair: LL-37 recruits neu­trophils, mono­cytes and T cells through FPR2, for­merly called FPRL1, and drives ker­atinocyte

migra­tion by trans­ac­ti­vat­ing EGFR. This links direct host defense with immune-cell recruit­ment and re-epithe­lial­iza­tion. Mouse stud­ies using an LL-37 plas­mid or pep­tide-loaded nanopar­ti­cles reported faster repair, neo­vas­cu­lar­iza­tion and changes in VEGF or inflam­ma­tory sig­nal­ing. These local-deliv­ery sys­tems define the expo­sure in those bar­rier-repair exper­i­ments.1575–1578

Endoge­nous Cathe­li­cidin Induc­tion: Human-cell exper­i­ments showed 1,25-dihy­drox­yvi­t­a­min D3 induc­tion of CAMP through a vit­a­min-D-response ele­ment occu­pied by the vit­a­min D recep­tor. The tested pro­moter response was present in pri­mates but absent in mice, lim­it­ing cross-species infer­ence.1579 Toll-like-recep­tor acti­va­tion in human macrophages increased both the vit­a­min D recep­tor and vit­a­min D-1-hydrox­y­lase. Cathe­li­cidin induc­tion and intra­cel­lu­lar Mycobac­terium tuber­cu­lo­sis killing fol­lowed. This sup­ports study of reg­u­lated endoge­nous pro­duc­tion, but it does not make vit­a­min D sup­ple­men­ta­tion equiv­a­lent to admin­is­tered LL-37 or estab­lish infec­tion treat­ment in patients.1580

Skin Pro­cess­ing and Rosacea: The bal­ance between pre­cur­sor acti­va­tion and fur­ther cleav­age can change cathe­li­cidin func­tion. Kallikreins 5 and 7 process hCAP18 and gen­er­ate shorter pep­tides with dif­fer­ent bio­log­i­cal activ­ity. Human rosacea skin con­tained excess cathe­li­cidin and processed forms unlike those in normal skin, asso­ci­ated with increased pro­tease activ­ity. Increased ker­atinocyte TLR2 sig­nal­ing pro­moted cal­cium-depen­dent kallikrein-5 release. These obser­va­tions con­nect exag­ger­ated envi­ron­men­tal sens­ing to inflam­ma­tory pep­tide pro­cess­ing and moti­vate inves­ti­ga­tion of that reg­u­la­tory path­way, rather than addi­tion of LL-37 as a rosacea treat­ment.1581–1583

Antibac­te­r­ial and Biofilm Appli­ca­tions: LL-37 can change bac­te­r­ial attach­ment, motil­ity and com­mu­nity orga­ni­za­tion below growth-inhibitory con­cen­tra­tions, cre­at­ing an antibiofilm research path dis­tinct from direct killing. The tissue-repair LL-37 entry gives the Pseudomonas con­cen­tra­tions and quorum-sens­ing find­ings in the wound con­text.41

Phago­cy­to­sis and Fungal Defense: LL-37 can assist micro­bial clear­ance with­out rely­ing entirely on mem­brane killing. Human macrophage exper­i­ments found increased uptake of bac­te­ria, with FPR2/ALX and TLR4-asso­ci­ated mech­a­nisms and increased Fc-gamma-recep­tor expres­sion. Sep­a­rate work iden­ti­fied LL-37 as a Mac-1 ligand that can coat bac­te­ria and pro­mote phago­cy­to­sis, illus­trat­ing more than one recep­tor-depen­dent route to host defense.1584,1585 Against Can­dida albi­cans, LL-37 bound cell-wall car­bo­hy­drates and inhib­ited adhe­sion at con­cen­tra­tions that did not kill the fungus. This was observed on plas­tic, cul­tured oral cells and mouse blad­der tissue. Other exper­i­ments demon­strated fungal mem­brane per­me­abi­liza­tion. Anti­ad­he­sion, direct killing and phago­cyte recruit­ment there­fore offer com­ple­men­tary research approaches to mucosal infec­tion.1586,1587

Endo­toxin and Sepsis-Asso­ci­ated Injury: Bind­ing micro­bial prod­ucts and alter­ing host responses could limit injury even when direct bac­te­r­ial killing is incom­plete. In rat endo­toxin, live-E. coli and cecal-lig­a­tion/punc­ture models, LL-37 reduced cir­cu­lat­ing endo­toxin and inflam­ma­tory read­outs and improved sur­vival. The latter model included both imme­di­ate and six-hour-delayed treat­ment. These exper­i­ments dis­tin­guish inflam­ma­tory con­trol from antibac­te­r­ial potency.1588 In human A549 epithe­lial cells and a mouse cecal-lig­a­tion/punc­ture model, a later study asso­ci­ated pro­tec­tion with reduced ZBP1 expres­sion and restored autophagy, with mech­a­nis­tic per­tur­ba­tions weak­en­ing the pro­tec­tive response. This pro­vides a pro­posed route link­ing micro­bial stress, oxida­tive injury and epithe­lial preser­va­tion during sepsis-asso­ci­ated lung injury.1589

Res­pi­ra­tory Antivi­ral Defense: In RSV sys­tems, LL-37 dis­rupted the viral enve­lope, reduced epithe­lial attach­ment and infec­tion, and pro­tected mice most strongly when given with the virus. Higher nasal LL-37 cor­re­lated with pro­tec­tion in a human chal­lenge study, which con­cerns endoge­nous defense rather than admin­is­tered treat­ment. Human LL-37 and mouse CRAMP also reduced repli­ca­tion and dis­ease sever­ity in influenza-infected mice.1590 Other influenza exper­i­ments reported direct virion and mem­brane effects with­out the same attach­ment-block­ing pat­tern. Serum and HDL weak­ened activ­ity. Virus, timing and bio­log­i­cal fluid there­fore shape local res­pi­ra­tory and ana­logue-devel­op­ment prospects.1591,1592,43

HIV and Cell-Con­text Depen­dence: LL-37 inhib­ited HIV-1 repli­ca­tion in periph­eral-blood mononu­clear cells and pri­mary CD4 T cells with­out detectable HIV-recep­tor down­reg­u­la­tion, and inde­pen­dently of FPRL1 sig­nal­ing. In a dif­fer­ent set­ting, HSV-2-infected epithe­lial cells pro­duced LL-37 that increased HIV-recep­tor expres­sion and sus­cep­ti­bil­ity in mono­cyte-derived Langer­hans cells. Block­ing LL-37 pro­duc­tion reduced the effect. The oppos­ing results make target-cell iden­tity and coin­fec­tion impor­tant to antivi­ral-devel­op­ment strate­gies, rather than sup­port­ing a uni­formly pro­tec­tive mucosal effect.1593,1594

Coro­n­avirus Entry and Mem­brane Tar­gets: LL-37 bound the SARS-CoV-2 spike recep­tor-bind­ing domain and ACE2 in lab­o­ra­tory exper­i­ments, inhib­ited pseudovirus, and reduced pseudovirus lung infec­tion after intranasal admin­is­tra­tion to human-ACE2-express­ing mice. Sep­a­rate cell work asso­ci­ated neu­tral­iza­tion of mul­ti­ple vari­ants with mem­brane dis­rup­tion and found greater activ­ity with niaci­namide in the tested for­mu­la­tion. That study also linked lower sali­vary LL-37 with greater COVID-19 sever­ity and found niaci­namide enhance­ment in saliva and skin-scrub sam­ples. The patient asso­ci­a­tion reflects endoge­nous pep­tide levels, not a treat­ment effect. These entry-inter­fer­ence and enve­lope-damage mech­a­nisms sup­port local-for­mu­la­tion research.1595,1596

Self-Nucleic-Acid Sens­ing and Autoim­mune Inflam­ma­tion: LL-37 can con­dense self nucleic acids and carry them to intra­cel­lu­lar sen­sors. In pso­ri­a­sis-related work, LL-37-self-DNA com­plexes reached plas­ma­cy­toid den­dritic-cell TLR9 com­part­ments and trig­gered type I inter­feron.1597 LL-37 also shielded self-RNA from degra­da­tion and deliv­ered it to den­dritic cells. These RNA com­plexes acti­vated TLR7 and inter­feron-alpha in plas­ma­cy­toid cells, while TLR8 acti­va­tion in myeloid den­dritic cells drove inflam­ma­tory cytokines and mat­u­ra­tion. The pep­tide can there­fore amplify ster­ile inflam­ma­tion by trans­port­ing mol­e­cules released from dam­aged cells.1598

Two sys­temic-lupus stud­ies pro­vide dif­fer­ent levels of evi­dence. One iso­lated LL-37- and HNP-con­tain­ing self-DNA immune com­plexes from sera of patients with high anti-DNA titers and showed that the com­plexes induced plas­ma­cy­toid-den­dritic-cell inter­feron-alpha through TLR9. Neu­trophil extra­cel­lu­lar traps released by patient neu­trophils also acti­vated those cells, link­ing antimi­cro­bial-pep­tide pro­tec­tion and trans­port of self-DNA to a dis­ease mech­a­nism with­out demon­strat­ing that LL-37 inhi­bi­tion would treat lupus.1599 In a sep­a­rate cohort, anti-LL-37 anti­bod­ies were higher in sys­temic lupus than in healthy and rheuma­toid-arthri­tis com­para­tors but did not cor­re­late with dis­ease activ­ity or organ involve­ment. After pris­tane expo­sure, cathe­li­cidin-defi­cient and wild-type mice did not differ sig­nif­i­cantly in mea­sured autoan­ti­bod­ies, cytokines, inter­feron-stim­u­lated-gene expres­sion, or lung and kidney pathol­ogy. The human asso­ci­a­tion and exper­i­men­tal null find­ings there­fore do not estab­lish that block­ing LL-37 alone would con­trol lupus.1600

Inflam­ma­tory ampli­fi­ca­tion also occurs out­side den­dritic cells. In human neu­trophils, NET-asso­ci­ated RNA-LL37 com­plexes pro­moted fur­ther NET for­ma­tion through TLR8-NLRP3 sig­nal­ing.1601 In pri­mary human ker­atinocytes, com­plexes of LL-37 with double-stranded RNA acti­vated MDA5-MAVS, cas­pase-3 and gas­der­min E and released pre­cur­sor IL-36-gamma, which still needed extra­cel­lu­lar pro­cess­ing for full activ­ity. These path­ways are selec­tive research tar­gets that coex­ist with LL-37’s antimi­cro­bial func­tions.1602

Tumor Microen­vi­ron­ment and Inves­ti­ga­tional Immunother­apy: LL-37 can influ­ence both tumor cells and the cells sup­port­ing them. In ovar­ian tumor models it recruited mes­enchy­mal stro­mal cells that sup­plied proan­gio­genic and immune-reg­u­la­tory fac­tors. Neu­tral­iza­tion reduced stro­mal recruit­ment and xenograft growth. Con­versely, colon-cancer cell exper­i­ments linked LL-37 to p53-asso­ci­ated, cas­pase-inde­pen­dent apop­to­sis, while loss of endoge­nous mouse cathe­li­cidin increased sus­cep­ti­bil­ity to chem­i­cally induced colon tumors. These obser­va­tions moti­vate con­text-spe­cific research on tumor sup­port, cell death and immune acti­va­tion rather than a gen­eral anti­cancer or cancer-pro­mot­ing clas­si­fi­ca­tion.1603,1604

An intra­tu­moral LL-37 melanoma study, NCT02225366, records four par­tic­i­pants enrolled, while its posted out­come cohorts rep­re­sent three par­tic­i­pants. Its sec­ondary end­point defined response as an immune-related com­plete or par­tial response and recorded counts of two and one in its two cohorts. How­ever, par­tic­i­pant flow also lists one first-cohort with­drawal for lack of effi­cacy, with only one par­tic­i­pant in each cohort com­plet­ing. That inter­nal incon­sis­tency pre­vents treat­ing the out­come table as three con­firmed respon­ders. The small uncon­trolled record does not estab­lish an effi­cacy esti­mate or an opti­mal bio­log­i­cal dose.1605 A pub­lished case described shrink­age of injected melanoma lesions fol­lowed by wide­spread lichenoid and atyp­i­cal squa­mous skin lesions, which resolved after LL-37 was stopped. This pro­vides a sep­a­rately attrib­uted response and tox­i­c­ity obser­va­tion with­out resolv­ing the reg­istry incon­sis­tency.1606

Human Top­i­cal Wound Evi­dence: Con­trolled top­i­cal stud­ies did not pro­duce a uni­form wound-heal­ing result. In a 34-par­tic­i­pant venous-leg-ulcer trial, twice-weekly LL-37 at 0.5 or 1.6 mg/mL pro­duced con­cen­tra­tion-spe­cific heal­ing sig­nals over four weeks, whereas 3.2 mg/mL did not differ from placebo.1607 A later 148-par­tic­i­pant phase IIb trial using 0.5 or 1.6 mg/mL twice weekly for 13 weeks found no sig­nif­i­cant heal­ing ben­e­fit in the full cohort. Its appar­ent ben­e­fit in ulcers at least 10 cm2 came from a post hoc sub­group analy­sis. Both con­cen­tra­tions were reported as well tol­er­ated.38 In a 25-par­tic­i­pant dia­betic-foot-ulcer trial, 0.5 mg/mL LL-37 cream accel­er­ated improve­ment in the gran­u­la­tion index but did not sig­nif­i­cantly reduce wound area or bac­te­r­ial colony counts. This sup­ports a gran­u­la­tion find­ing with­out demon­strat­ing antimi­cro­bial clear­ance.40 The tissue-repair LL-37 entry pro­vides the fuller dose-spe­cific account, includ­ing neg­a­tive out­comes and tol­er­a­bil­ity

Practical Considerations

Inflam­ma­tory Response and Expo­sure: Human cell stud­ies show LL-37 acti­va­tion of eosinophils with cys­teinyl-leukotriene release and mast-cell degran­u­la­tion

through MRGPRX2. Its host-defense activ­ity can there­fore also amplify inflam­ma­tion. Antivi­ral or repair devel­op­ment must account for con­cen­tra­tion, site and for­mu­la­tion. Tol­er­a­bil­ity of top­i­cal wound use cannot estab­lish sys­temic tol­er­a­bil­ity.1608,1609

Matrix, Pro­cess­ing and Airway Effects: Gly­cosamino­gly­can-rich wound fluids and heparin-con­tain­ing plasma inhib­ited antibac­te­r­ial activ­ity in lab­o­ra­tory assays, so applied con­cen­tra­tion and mea­sured total pep­tide need not equal freely active LL-37. Trun­cated LL-37 ana­logues also dif­fered in antimi­cro­bial, chemoat­trac­tant, endo­toxin-neu­tral­iz­ing and cyto­toxic effects. Parent pep­tide and frag­ments cannot be treated as inter­change­able.44,1610 Human COPD airway sam­ples showed an asso­ci­a­tion between LL-37 and MUC5AC, while airway-cell exper­i­ments linked LL-37 to mucin induc­tion through TACE/EGFR/ERK and IL-8. This adds mucus ampli­fi­ca­tion to the expo­sure con­sid­er­a­tions for pul­monary host-defense strate­gies.1611

Clin­i­cal Local Expo­sure: The melanoma reg­istry describes weekly intra­tu­moral cohorts of 250 micro­grams per tumor injec­tion in two par­tic­i­pants and 500 micro­grams in one, with adverse-event col­lec­tion during up to eight weeks of injec­tions. It records no deaths or seri­ous adverse events in the stated obser­va­tion win­dows, but other events in all three par­tic­i­pants, includ­ing squa­mous-cell car­ci­noma and actinic ker­ato­sis in the first cohort and anemia in both cohorts. These are observed events with­out estab­lished pep­tide causal­ity. The trial-derived expo­sures and the top­i­cal wound con­cen­tra­tions describe dif­fer­ent local prod­ucts and indi­ca­tions. Nei­ther defines a sys­temic LL-37 range.1605

Prod­uct Char­ac­ter­i­za­tion: FDA’s cap­tured com­pound­ing page places LL-37 among sub­stances whose nom­i­na­tions were with­drawn after ear­lier cat­e­gory 2 place­ment under interim policy. It cites lim­ited human safety infor­ma­tion, pos­si­ble immuno­genic­ity, pep­tide impu­ri­ties and active-ingre­di­ent-char­ac­ter­i­za­tion con­cerns, plus non­clin­i­cal male-repro­duc­tive and tissue-spe­cific pro­tu­mori­genic find­ings. These prod­uct risks remain rel­e­vant beside antimi­cro­bial, antivi­ral and repair hypothe­ses.45

10Peptide Backbone Engineering and Membrane Permeability

Peptide Backbone Engineering and Membrane Permeability

Back­bone and side-chain changes can alter pas­sive dif­fu­sion, cytoso­lic accu­mu­la­tion, pro­te­olytic sta­bil­ity, recep­tor phar­ma­col­ogy and tissue dis­tri­b­u­tion in

dif­fer­ent direc­tions. The useful prop­erty depends on the appli­ca­tion, and every result remains spe­cific to the tested struc­ture, assay and expo­sure.1612.1613–1615

Applications

Oral Deliv­ery And Con­for­ma­tional Switch­ing: A de novo design study made 184 macro­cy­cles con­tain­ing 6 to 12 residues and exper­i­men­tally deter­mined

35 struc­tures. Eighty-four designs exceeded an appar­ent per­me­abil­ity of 1 × 10^-6 cm/s. Inter­nal hydro­gen bond­ing shielded back­bone polar groups. For designs with exposed NH groups, an alter­na­tive isoen­er­getic fully hydro­gen-bonded state was favored in the lipid mem­brane. A sep­a­rate 8,448-member cyclic-pep­tide screen pro­duced throm­bin binders with nanomo­lar affin­ity, high sta­bil­ity and oral bioavail­abil­ity as high as 18% in rats. These results con­nect mem­brane pas­sage to oral expo­sure for the exact tested macro­cy­cles. They do not estab­lish oral deliv­ery for an untested pep­tide.1612.1616

Mem­brane Access And Target Recog­ni­tion: A 2026 com­par­i­son found that a chloroalkene dipep­tide isostere pro­duced the largest improve­ment in pas­sive dif­fu­sion and cel­lu­lar uptake among the back­bone changes tested. The authors attrib­uted the effect mainly to increased lipophilic­ity and reduced hydra­tion rather than con­for­ma­tional change. The sub­sti­tu­tion was extended to longer polar sequences and cyclic scaf­folds. In G-quadru­plex-bind­ing RGG pep­tides, it pre­served or improved RNA affin­ity and selec­tiv­ity, show­ing why access and intended bind­ing need sep­a­rate mea­sure­ment.1613

Sta­bil­ity With Pre­served Antag­o­nism: In hydro­car­bon-sta­pled CGRP-recep­tor antag­o­nists, one pep­tide with an S5/S5 i,i+4 staple at posi­tions 12 and 16 improved serum sta­bil­ity while retain­ing antag­o­nism. Aib36-con­tain­ing pep­tides remained intact during pro­longed serum expo­sure but lost antag­o­nism. Com­pu­ta­tional pre­dic­tions of helic­ity and recep­tor bind­ing largely matched exper­i­men­tal out­comes, but the cor­re­spon­dence was incon­sis­tent. Sta­bil­ity and recep­tor func­tion still required exper­i­men­tal con­fir­ma­tion.1614

Intra­cel­lu­lar Pro­tein Inter­ac­tions: A series of more than 350 sta­pled pep­tides tar­get­ing Mdm2 and MdmX com­pared bind­ing, per­me­abil­ity, sol­u­bil­ity, cel­lu­lar activ­ity and target-inde­pen­dent effects. Across the tested series, lipophilic­ity tracked per­me­abil­ity, charge affected off-target tox­i­c­ity or sol­u­bil­ity, C-ter­mi­nal fea­tures affected potency, and staple fea­tures affected polyphar­ma­col­ogy. The selected pep­tides gained cel­lu­lar potency with­out off-target cell-pro­lif­er­a­tion effects. This work sup­ports a screen­ing frame­work for an intra­cel­lu­lar pro­tein inter­ac­tion, not a uni­ver­sal rule that any staple con­fers useful cell entry.1617

Tar­geted Tissue Dis­tri­b­u­tion: Flu­o­res­cent PSMA-tar­geted lig­ands were tested in cells and mice. A con­ju­gate with two zwit­te­ri­onic groups in its linker retained spe­cific PSMA affin­ity, IC50 4.39 ± 1.69 nM, and uptake in tumor cells and xenografts while show­ing very low off-target tissue reten­tion. The authors pro­posed trans­fer of this linker strat­egy to other tar­get­ing vec­tors and effec­tors, includ­ing radio­phar­ma­ceu­ti­cals. That exten­sion remains prospec­tive.1615

Pre­clin­i­cal Recep­tor And Func­tional Effects: In a 16-ana­logue cyclic opioid hexa­pep­tide series, changes at Gly3 sep­a­rated mixed MOR and KOR ago­nism with DOR antag­o­nism from selec­tive MOR antag­o­nism. After periph­eral admin­is­tra­tion in ani­mals, one ana­logue pro­duced potent antinoci­cep­tion. The authors also reported reduced antinoci­cep­tive tol­er­ance, phys­i­cal depen­dence and res­pi­ra­tory depres­sion. Another ana­logue atten­u­ated fen­tanyl-induced antinoci­cep­tive tol­er­ance through periph­eral MOR block­ade. These are pre­clin­i­cal func­tional obser­va­tions for named ana­logues, not human effi­cacy or a gen­eral pep­tide-engi­neer­ing recipe.1618

Practical Considerations

Dis­tinct Per­me­abil­ity End­points: Pas­sive dif­fu­sion, whole-cell uptake, cytoso­lic accu­mu­la­tion, intact-pep­tide per­sis­tence, recep­tor response and tissue reten­tion

answer dif­fer­ent ques­tions. A sta­bil­ity gain with­out retained activ­ity, or increased cell asso­ci­a­tion with­out cytoso­lic access, does not estab­lish a useful intra­cel­lu­lar agent.1613.1614,1619

Cytoso­lic-Accu­mu­la­tion Assay: The 2026 CHAMP assay uses HaloTag-express­ing mam­malian cells, strain-pro­moted azide-alkyne cycload­di­tion and azide-tagged test mol­e­cules to dis­tin­guish cytoso­lic accu­mu­la­tion from mem­brane-asso­ci­ated or endo­so­ma­lly trapped mate­r­ial. In cell-pen­e­trat­ing pep­tides, longer pol­yargi­nine sequences accu­mu­lated more, while C-ter­mi­nal ami­da­tion and D-amino-acid sub­sti­tu­tions also affected cel­lu­lar pen­e­tra­tion. Because the mea­sure­ment depends on a tagged assay format and engi­neered cells, its result should be inter­preted as one com­part­ment-spe­cific screen rather than direct evi­dence of intact target engage­ment in vivo.1619

Selec­tiv­ity and Trans­la­tion: Lipophilic­ity and pos­i­tive charge can improve entry while wors­en­ing sol­u­bil­ity, mem­brane dis­rup­tion or target-inde­pen­dent activ­ity. The Mdm2/MdmX work there­fore used mem­brane-integrity and off-target coun­ter­screens along­side cel­lu­lar potency. Sequence-spe­cific effi­cacy, sys­temic expo­sure, tissue dis­tri­b­u­tion and tox­i­c­ity remain nec­es­sary before an engi­neered pep­tide can be trans­lated from an assay or animal model.1617.1615,1618

11Microneedle Patch Platforms for Peptide Cargos

Microneedle Patch Platforms for Peptide Cargos

Micronee­dle devices bypass the stra­tum corneum by form­ing tran­sient micro­p­ores or plac­ing cargo within the skin. Pep­tide and pro­tein uses range from ana­bolic

hor­mones and insulin to vac­cine anti­gen, coun­ter­reg­u­la­tory glucagon, and inves­ti­ga­tional semaglu­tide deliv­ery. Device mate­r­ial, cargo sta­bil­ity, actual sys­temic expo­sure, skin recov­ery, and the clin­i­cal end­point must be judged for each plat­form rather than inferred from the needle format alone.1620–1624

Applications

Vac­ci­na­tion: In a ran­dom­ized Phase 1 study of 100 adults, dis­solv­ing influenza-vac­cine patches were com­pared with intra­mus­cu­lar vac­cine and placebo patch,

includ­ing par­tic­i­pant self-admin­is­tra­tion. Day-28 anti­body responses were sim­i­lar between vac­ci­nated groups. No treat­ment-related seri­ous adverse event occurred. Itch­ing, ery­thema, and ten­der­ness at the patch site were common, mostly mild, and tran­sient. The trial estab­lished immuno­genic­ity, tol­er­a­bil­ity, and accept­abil­ity, not clin­i­cal pre­ven­tion of influenza.1620

Teri­paratide Deliv­ery: A six-month ran­dom­ized osteo­poro­sis study enrolled 165 post­menopausal women. Trans­der­mal teri­paratide pro­duced dose-related lumbar-spine bone-min­eral-den­sity gains versus placebo patch, and the 40-micro­gram patch also improved total-hip den­sity rel­a­tive to placebo and the injec­tion com­para­tor. Related phar­ma­co­ki­netic work showed an ear­lier, briefer plasma pulse than injec­tion. Bone den­sity and turnover were mea­sured. Frac­ture reduc­tion was not.1621,1622

Abaloparatide Deliv­ery: In an open-label Phase 1b fea­si­bil­ity study, 22 post­menopausal women self-admin­is­tered a microstruc­tured system for 29 days. First-appli­ca­tion suc­cess was 99.7%, expo­sure was con­sis­tent across visits, and median PINP rose 64.4% by day 29. No seri­ous adverse event or adverse-event with­drawal occurred, and local reac­tions were pre­dom­i­nantly mild or mod­er­ate.1625

The later Phase 3 com­par­i­son enrolled 511 post­menopausal women for 12 months. Lumbar-spine bone den­sity rose 7.14% with the abaloparatide patch and 10.86% with sub­cu­ta­neous drug, a dif­fer­ence of -3.72 per­cent­age points that failed the pre­spec­i­fied non­in­fe­ri­or­ity test. Admin­is­tra­tion-site reac­tions occurred in 94.4% and 70.5%, respec­tively, while seri­ous-adverse-event fre­quency was sim­i­lar. Both routes were active, but the mea­sured effects were not equiv­a­lent.1626

Insulin Absorp­tion: A five-person type 1 dia­betes study found that micronee­dle intra­der­mal lispro reached peak con­cen­tra­tion in about half the time of sub­cu­ta­neous admin­is­tra­tion and low­ered post-meal glu­cose more. A later crossover study enrolled 28 people, with 23 com­plet­ing both three-day peri­ods, and com­pared intra­der­mal with sub­cu­ta­neous insulin-aspart infu­sion. Faster peak insulin and lower early post­pran­dial glycemia per­sisted across all three days. These were infu­sion sys­tems rather than dis­solv­ing drug-loaded patches.1623,1624

Glu­cose-Respon­sive Glucagon: An exper­i­men­tal hydro­gel released zinc-glucagon as glu­cose fell through com­pet­i­tive cat­e­chol and boronic-acid inter­ac­tions. In dia­betic male rats it pre­vented hypo­glycemia for as long as six hours during two con­sec­u­tive insulin chal­lenges. This is a pre­ven­tive coun­ter­reg­u­la­tory system, dis­tinct from faster insulin absorp­tion and from man­u­ally admin­is­tered rescue glucagon.1627

Dual-Hor­mone Sys­tems: A hybrid patch with sep­a­rate insulin and glucagon mod­ules low­ered high glu­cose while reduc­ing hypo­glycemia in dia­betic mice sub­jected to delayed meals or insulin over­dose. Another charge-respon­sive plat­form paired insulin with a pos­i­tively charged glucagon ana­logue and reg­u­lated glu­cose in dia­betic mice and minip­igs, last­ing more than 24 hours in the minipig exper­i­ments. These devices attempt to repro­duce oppos­ing pan­cre­atic responses, but the matrix, mod­i­fied cargo, and animal model define each result.1628,1629

Other Hor­mone Cargos: Two-layer dis­solv­ing micronee­dles deliv­ered desmo­pressin and recom­bi­nant human growth hor­mone through rat skin, with sys­temic absorp­tion for both and faster uptake of desmo­pressin. Coated salmon-cal­ci­tonin patches in hair­less rats pro­duced much higher expo­sure than nasal deliv­ery and did not differ sig­nif­i­cantly from sub­cu­ta­neous injec­tion. In a sep­a­rate dis­solv­ing-array study, tre­halose improved cal­ci­tonin sta­bil­ity under heat and humid­ity. These stud­ies demon­strate cargo accom­mo­da­tion, expo­sure, and for­mu­la­tion sta­bil­ity rather than one gen­eral release sched­ule.1630–1632

Semaglu­tide Devel­op­ment: NCT07673900 was recruit­ing on 2026-09-10 for a ran­dom­ized Phase 1 com­par­i­son of a semaglu­tide microar­ray patch with sub­cu­ta­neous admin­is­tra­tion, with esti­mated enroll­ment of 62 and no results posted. A sep­a­rate 2026 dis­solv­ing-micronee­dle paper reported mate­r­ial char­ac­ter­i­za­tion, inser­tion into ex vivo porcine skin, release test­ing, and flu­o­res­cent-dye depo­si­tion. It did not report human semaglu­tide phar­ma­co­ki­net­ics or weight out­comes.1633,1634

Practical Considerations

Patch Con­tent and Deliv­ered Expo­sure: The teri­paratide trial used patches nom­i­nally con­tain­ing 20, 30, or 40 micro­grams for 30 min­utes daily and a 20-micro­gram

daily sub­cu­ta­neous com­para­tor. Patch con­tent and injected dose are not equiv­a­lent mea­sures of deliv­ered expo­sure. Phar­ma­co­ki­netic test­ing found dose-pro­por­tional patch expo­sure with vari­abil­ity com­pa­ra­ble to injec­tion, but a dif­fer­ent con­cen­tra­tion-time pro­file.1621,1622

Cargo-Spe­cific Stor­age Sta­bil­ity: In the rat two-layer study, desmo­pressin and growth hor­mone remained stable in devices for one month at 4°C and -80°C. Those stor­age find­ings answer a dif­fer­ent ques­tion from heat-and-humid­ity sta­bi­liza­tion of cal­ci­tonin with tre­halose. Shelf life cannot be trans­ferred across cargo, matrix, pro­cess­ing, and stor­age con­di­tions.1630,1632

Micro­p­ore Per­sis­tence: Micro­p­ore per­sis­tence is another device-spe­cific vari­able. In 111 healthy par­tic­i­pants pre­treated on the upper arm under occlu­sive cov­er­ings, mean clo­sure esti­mates ranged from 44.1 to 66.5 hours. Clo­sure was longer in darker skin, with mea­sured skin color out­per­form­ing self-iden­ti­fied race or eth­nic­ity as a pre­dic­tor. These data belong to the tested pre­treat­ment and cov­er­ing con­di­tions. They do not give a uni­ver­sal recov­ery time or an observed infec­tion rate for all micronee­dle sys­tems.1635

12BNP Molecular Forms for Cardiac Risk Stratification

BNP Molecular Forms for Cardiac Risk Stratification

BNP-related tests detect prod­ucts from a het­ero­ge­neous syn­the­sis and pro­cess­ing path­way. The pre­cur­sor proBNP1-108, bio­log­i­cally active BNP32, inac­tive

amino-ter­mi­nal frag­ments, gly­co­sy­lated forms and trun­cated pep­tides differ in activ­ity and in recog­ni­tion by par­tic­u­lar assays. Mech­a­nis­tic stud­ies of these forms and clin­i­cal evi­dence for named BNP, NT-proBNP or MR-proANP assays there­fore answer related but dis­tinct ques­tions.1636.1637,1638

Applications

Pro­cess­ing And Assay Biol­ogy: In cell models, O-gly­co­sy­la­tion at Thr71 and cleav­age by furin or corin changed with the cell system. Among 53 people

hos­pi­tal­ized with heart fail­ure, those with higher aortic plasma levels of immunore­ac­tive BNP had more severe dis­ease, a greater pro­por­tion of inac­tive proBNP among the immunore­ac­tive forms secreted from the heart, and a weaker esti­mated bio­log­i­cal response. Mech­a­nis­tic exper­i­ments linked sup­pres­sion of myocar­dial miR-30 to GALNT1/2-medi­ated gly­co­sy­la­tion at Thr48 and Thr71, reduced pro­cess­ing and greater secre­tion of inac­tive proBNP.1636.1637

Mol­e­c­u­lar-Form Prog­no­sis: A prospec­tive cohort mea­sured proBNP1-108 and BNP32 in 756 out­pa­tients with pre­dom­i­nantly sys­tolic heart fail­ure. Com­par­ing the third with first proBNP1-108 ter­tile, the adjusted hazard ratio for all-cause death or car­diac trans­plan­ta­tion was 4.9 (95% CI 2.5 to 9.7). ProBNP1-108 added risk infor­ma­tion par­tic­u­larly when BNP32 was below the cohort median of 125 pg/mL. This obser­va­tional asso­ci­a­tion does not estab­lish rou­tine mol­e­c­u­lar pro­fil­ing or prove that acting on the result improves out­comes.1639

Clin­i­cal Assess­ment And Prog­no­sis: The 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guide­line sup­ports BNP or NT-proBNP mea­sure­ment during eval­u­a­tion of dys­p­nea, for risk strat­i­fi­ca­tion in chronic heart fail­ure, and for prog­no­sis at hos­pi­tal admis­sion. A pre­dis­charge mea­sure­ment can add post­dis­charge prog­nos­tic infor­ma­tion. For selected people at risk, it also sup­ports BNP- or NT-proBNP-based screen­ing fol­lowed by coor­di­nated car­dio­vas­cu­lar care. Any ben­e­fit belongs to the com­bined test­ing-and-care strat­egy rather than the assay alone.1640

In a prospec­tive 658-person chest-pain cohort, pre­sen­ta­tion NT-proBNP mod­estly improved diag­nos­tic dis­crim­i­na­tion when added to car­diac tro­ponin T and inde­pen­dently pre­dicted longer-term mor­tal­ity. A sep­a­rate prospec­tive study of 1,105 people under­go­ing emer­gency PCI for acute myocar­dial infarc­tion found that serial NT-proBNP improved selected prog­nos­tic models. Sev­eral day-2, day-3, day-7 and peak asso­ci­a­tions with later major car­dio­vas­cu­lar events were U-shaped. These stud­ies con­cern adjunc­tive risk esti­ma­tion, not proof that bio­marker-directed treat­ment improves out­comes.1641.1642

Meta­bolic And Com­par­a­tive Research: In a com­mu­nity study of nine natri­uretic-pep­tide vari­ants, only NT-proANP and NT-proBNP inde­pen­dently pre­dicted insulin resis­tance in both sexes. Pre­cur­sor-to-bioac­tive ratios did not sup­port increased pep­tide clear­ance as the expla­na­tion. Copeptin-plus-tro­ponin stud­ies are a sep­a­rate early myocar­dial-infarc­tion rule-out strat­egy and do not estab­lish a BNP mol­e­c­u­lar-form appli­ca­tion.1643.1644,1645

Practical Considerations

Thresh­olds and Clin­i­cal Con­text: In nona­cute assess­ment, the 2021 ESC guide­line states that heart fail­ure is unlikely below BNP 35 pg/mL, NT-proBNP 125

pg/mL or MR-proANP 40 pmol/L. Figure 6 in the retained 2022 Euro­pean Jour­nal of Heart Fail­ure co-pub­li­ca­tion gives acute rule-out values below BNP 100 pg/mL and NT-proBNP 300 pg/mL, and age-spe­cific NT-proBNP rule-in values above 450 pg/mL for age under 55, above 900 pg/mL for ages 55 to 75, and above 1,800 pg/mL for age over 75.2139 Thresh­olds have dif­fer­ent pur­poses, and age, atrial fib­ril­la­tion, kidney dis­ease, obe­sity and clin­i­cal set­ting alter inter­pre­ta­tion. These thresh­olds are his­tor­i­cal descrip­tions from the cited 2021 guide­line pub­li­ca­tions. ESC released a 2026 heart-fail­ure guide­line, but con­cor­dance of its cor­re­spond­ing values, units and age bands, includ­ing those for MR-proANP, remains unver­i­fied as of 2026-09-13. The 2021 fig­ures are not pre­sented here as cur­rent ESC guid­ance.1646.3817

Figure 6 and adja­cent text in the retained 2022 Euro­pean Jour­nal of Heart Fail­ure co-pub­li­ca­tion lit­er­ally print an acute MR-proANP thresh­old of 120 pg/mL. The pri­mary 1,641-person BACH study that estab­lished non­in­fe­ri­or­ity to BNP used 120 pmol/L. No ver­i­fied offi­cial cor­rec­tion resolv­ing this con­flict was located. Mass and molar units are not inter­change­able with­out a valid assay-spe­cific con­ver­sion, so the two source state­ments remain explicit rather than silently nor­mal­ized.2139.1647

Bio­marker-Guided Ther­apy: GUIDE-IT ran­dom­ized 894 high-risk par­tic­i­pants with heart fail­ure and reduced ejec­tion frac­tion, 446 to NT-proBNP-guided care tar­get­ing below 1,000 pg/mL and 448 to usual guide­line-based care. Enroll­ment stopped for futil­ity before the planned 1,100. First heart-fail­ure hos­pi­tal­iza­tion or car­dio­vas­cu­lar death occurred in 164 par­tic­i­pants (37%) in each group, adjusted hazard ratio 0.98 (95% CI 0.79 to 1.22; P=0.88). The ran­dom­ized neg­a­tive result sep­a­rates prog­nos­tic asso­ci­a­tion from ben­e­fit obtained by treat­ing to a bio­marker target.1648

Sam­pling And Assay Speci­ficity: Mass spec­trom­e­try found active BNP1-32 con­cen­tra­tions 4.4-fold lower than BNP immunoas­say results in people with heart fail­ure. In 22 par­tic­i­pants, active BNP1-32 levels decreased by a reported 50% after a median of eight weeks of sacu­bi­tril/val­sar­tan. Indi­vid­ual changes spanned both direc­tions. A sep­a­rate study showed that BNP and NT-proBNP immunore­ac­tiv­i­ties com­prise dif­fer­ent pre­cur­sor, trun­cated and gly­co­sy­lated species, and that gly­co­sy­la­tion can alter mea­sured tra­jec­to­ries during treat­ment.1649.1638

In a diag­nos­tic study of 183 people with heart fail­ure and 200 with­out heart fail­ure, Elec­sys proBNP II was tested on untreated and gly­cosi­dase-treated sam­ples, while Super­Flex NT-proBNP was tested on untreated sam­ples. ROC AUCs were 0.943 for untreated Elec­sys proBNP II, 0.935 after gly­cosi­dase treat­ment and 0.930 for untreated Super­Flex NT-proBNP. Sim­i­lar per­for­mance in this one com­par­i­son does not estab­lish inter­change­abil­ity across plat­forms. Serial inter­pre­ta­tion should retain the assay, mol­e­c­u­lar species rec­og­nized, sam­pling time and treat­ment expo­sure.1650

13Neurogranin as Synaptic Loss Biomarker

Neurogranin as Synaptic Loss Biomarker

Neu­ro­granin, or RC3, is a 78-amino-acid post­sy­nap­tic calmod­ulin-bind­ing pro­tein involved in synap­tic sig­nal­ing. Intra­cel­lu­lar pro­tein abun­dance in brain and

extra­cel­lu­lar con­cen­tra­tion in cere­brospinal fluid (CSF) are bio­log­i­cally related but non­in­ter­change­able mea­sure­ments. The research record spans synap­tic func­tion, prog­no­sis, dif­fer­en­tial diag­no­sis, acute injury, psy­chi­atric and infec­tious con­texts, and mul­ti­marker inter­pre­ta­tion.1651–1655

Biological Functions

Synap­tic Struc­ture and Cel­lu­lar Restora­tion: Human post­mortem stud­ies found less brain neu­ro­granin in Alzheimer dis­ease and less synapse-asso­ci­ated

pro­tein in people with life­time cog­ni­tive decline. Restor­ing neu­ro­granin in pri­mary neu­rons pro­moted den­dritic growth, synapse number, coor­di­nated net­work activ­ity, and meta­bolic com­pe­tence through altered cal­cium and calmod­ulin sig­nal­ing. These exper­i­ments explain why the pro­tein can mark synap­tic biol­ogy, but intra­cel­lu­lar restora­tion does not deter­mine the mean­ing of a CSF value.1654,1656

Learn­ing and Behav­ioral Plas­tic­ity: Pre­frontal over­ex­pres­sion in trans­genic mice increased long-term poten­ti­a­tion and accel­er­ated extinc­tion of con­di­tioned fear and sucrose self-admin­is­tra­tion. This con­nects neu­ro­granin-depen­dent plas­tic­ity with updat­ing learned responses and moti­vates research in trauma-related and addic­tive dis­or­ders with­out demon­strat­ing a human treat­ment effect.1657

Tran­scrip­tional Reg­u­la­tion: The NRGN gene con­tains a thy­roid-hor­mone-respon­sive ele­ment and shows recep­tor-depen­dent tran­scrip­tional reg­u­la­tion. Chronic cocaine expo­sure in mice reduced neu­ro­granin expres­sion despite unchanged cir­cu­lat­ing thy­roid hor­mones, with retinoid and thy­roid-recep­tor sig­nal­ing impli­cated. Expres­sion reg­u­la­tion there­fore extends beyond release from injured synapses.1652,1658

Neurodegeneration and Cognitive Prognosis

Cog­ni­tive Prog­no­sis: Within the Alzheimer Dis­ease Neu­roimag­ing Ini­tia­tive, higher base­line CSF neu­ro­granin in mild cog­ni­tive impair­ment pre­dicted later

cog­ni­tive decline, lower cor­ti­cal glu­cose metab­o­lism, and hip­pocam­pal volume loss. A sep­a­rate lon­gi­tu­di­nal cohort asso­ci­ated base­line con­cen­tra­tion with future impair­ment in ini­tially normal par­tic­i­pants and with decline rates in symp­to­matic Alzheimer dis­ease. These obser­va­tional uses con­cern prog­no­sis and com­ple­ment pathol­ogy mark­ers rather than replac­ing them.1653,1659

Synap­tic-Den­sity Imag­ing: A small imag­ing com­par­i­son com­bined 21 early-Alzheimer par­tic­i­pants with seven cog­ni­tively normal par­tic­i­pants. Across the full sample, higher CSF neu­ro­granin cor­re­lated with lower global synap­tic den­sity on [11C]UCB-J SV2A PET. Within Alzheimer dis­ease, sig­nif­i­cant asso­ci­a­tions involved syn­taxin-7 and PEBP-1 instead. The cross-group neu­ro­granin asso­ci­a­tion does not estab­lish equiv­a­lent per­for­mance within one stage or a direct count of lost synapses.1660

Microglial and Synap­tic Tra­jec­to­ries: Lon­gi­tu­di­nal WRAP and ALFA+ cohorts linked higher base­line CSF sTREM2 with a smaller sub­se­quent neu­ro­granin increase. In WRAP, lower start­ing neu­ro­granin inde­pen­dently pre­dicted a larger later sTREM2 increase. These timing-depen­dent asso­ci­a­tions sup­port study of synap­tic and microglial inter­ac­tions in pre­clin­i­cal dis­ease but do not define a single favor­able direc­tion for either marker.1655

Diag­nos­tic Speci­ficity: Diag­nos­tic speci­ficity is lim­ited. Early work found higher CSF neu­ro­granin in Alzheimer dis­ease than most com­par­i­son groups except speech-vari­ant fron­totem­po­ral demen­tia. Later clin­i­cal and autopsy cohorts showed increases in some non-Alzheimer con­di­tions, par­tic­u­larly Creutzfeldt-Jakob dis­ease, and incon­sis­tent rela­tion­ships with Alzheimer neu­ropatho­logic stage. A mul­ti­cen­ter study found no improve­ment over core Alzheimer bio­mark­ers. Prog­nos­tic asso­ci­a­tion should there­fore remain sep­a­rate from eti­o­logic diag­no­sis.1661–1663

Other Neurologic Contexts

Parkin­son­ian Find­ings: Parkin­son­ian stud­ies dis­agree. One 30-patient and 30-con­trol study reported lower CSF neu­ro­granin and an

amy­loid-beta42-to-neu­ro­granin ratio with 92% sen­si­tiv­ity and 71.4% speci­ficity for cog­ni­tive impair­ment. Another linked lower neu­ro­granin with cor­ti­cal glu­cose metab­o­lism and cog­ni­tion. A larger cohort found no rela­tion­ship with cog­ni­tive or motor impair­ment, lon­gi­tu­di­nal decline, or demen­tia con­ver­sion, while a drug-naive sub­group study reported higher values with worse cog­ni­tion and motor stage. Dis­ease stage, treat­ment, assay, and intended end­point may account for some dif­fer­ences.1664–1667

Prion-Dis­ease Research: In spo­radic Creutzfeldt-Jakob dis­ease, a study com­pared 81 cases with 46 Alzheimer cases and 64 neu­ro­log­i­cal con­trols. CSF neu­ro­granin was high­est in prion dis­ease and sep­a­rated it from Alzheimer dis­ease with area under the curve 0.85. Values dif­fered by prion sub­type, cor­re­lated with tau, and asso­ci­ated with sur­vival, while brain-tissue abun­dance was reduced. Rapid injury there­fore pro­vides a dis­tinct diag­nos­tic and prog­nos­tic research set­ting.1668

Acute Brain Injury: Acute injury results depend on spec­i­men and assay. Serum was higher in 76 trau­matic-brain-injury cases than 150 con­trols but did not iden­tify intracra­nial hem­or­rhage. Reported medi­ans, 0.18 and 0.02 ng/mL, were below the stated 0.2 ng/mL quan­tifi­ca­tion limit. In acute ischemic stroke, a 50-patient paired-fluid study related neu­ro­granin chiefly to infarct volume, while tau better tracked sever­ity and out­come. Later serum stud­ies reported early diag­nos­tic dis­crim­i­na­tion with­out six-month prog­no­sis or only lim­ited dis­crim­i­na­tion, includ­ing an area under the curve of 0.624 in 44 cases and 44 con­trols.1669–1672

Seizure-Group Com­par­i­son: Serum neu­ro­granin was higher in 49 emer­gency-depart­ment seizure cases than 53 healthy con­trols. Although the authors pro­posed dis­tin­guish­ing epilep­tic from psy­chogenic nonepilep­tic events, no nonepilep­tic-seizure com­par­i­son group is reported in the abstract. The sup­ported appli­ca­tion is detec­tion of a group dif­fer­ence, not the pro­posed dif­fer­en­tial diag­no­sis.1673

Hunt­ing­ton-Dis­ease Find­ings: CSF neu­ro­granin did not differ sig­nif­i­cantly in Hunt­ing­ton dis­ease and did not assoc­iate with dis­ease burden, func­tional capac­ity, or motor score. This neg­a­tive result is infor­ma­tive because synap­tic dys­func­tion in a dis­ease does not guar­an­tee a useful extra­cel­lu­lar neu­ro­granin signal.1674

Psychiatric and Infection-Related Research

Psy­chi­atric Tissue and Fluid Stud­ies: Seven schiz­o­phre­nia post­mortem cases had less pre­frontal neu­ro­granin stain­ing than seven con­trols with­out a change in

pos­i­tively stained pyra­mi­dal-cell den­sity. By con­trast, 40 first-episode-psy­chosis patients and 20 con­trols showed no sig­nif­i­cant CSF group dif­fer­ence. Med­ica­tion expo­sure and smok­ing, but not symp­toms or cog­ni­tion, asso­ci­ated with con­cen­tra­tion. In 12 depressed patients, mean CSF neu­ro­granin did not change after ECT, though base­line values cor­re­lated with response. These tissue, state, and pre­dic­tion ques­tions do not sup­port an estab­lished psy­chi­atric test.1675–1677

Infec­tion-Related Find­ings: HIV-asso­ci­ated tissue and neu­ronal exper­i­ments linked inflam­ma­tory prod­ucts from infected macrophages with reduced neu­ro­granin and altered neu­ro­granin-calmod­ulin sig­nal­ing. An orig­i­nal CSF cohort nev­er­the­less found no sig­nif­i­cant sep­a­ra­tion among the exam­ined HIV groups and unin­fected con­trols. Plasma neu­ro­granin cor­re­lated with cog­ni­tive scores in pooled neu­rosyphilis and gen­eral-pare­sis par­tic­i­pants. Tissue mech­a­nism, CSF con­cen­tra­tion, and plasma asso­ci­a­tion are dis­tinct find­ings.1678–1680

Exploratory Anti­sense Reg­u­la­tion: An unre­viewed preprint pro­poses an addi­tional reg­u­la­tory layer: RP11-677M14.2, an anti­sense long non­cod­ing RNA, was inversely asso­ci­ated with neu­ro­granin expres­sion across HIV-related tissue, cell, and organoid exper­i­ments. This remains exploratory.1681

Panels, Vesicles, and Measurement

Extra­cel­lu­lar-Vesi­cle Panels: Blood frac­tions enriched for puta­tive neu­ronal extra­cel­lu­lar vesi­cles have shown lower neu­ro­granin and other synap­tic pro­teins in

Alzheimer-related panels, includ­ing before cog­ni­tive impair­ment. During ten years of obser­va­tion in 231 ini­tially normal older adults, higher phys­i­cal activ­ity was asso­ci­ated with less panel decline and slower cog­ni­tive loss in pre­clin­i­cal Alzheimer dis­ease. These results depend on enrich­ment meth­ods and are asso­ci­a­tion-based mon­i­tor­ing sig­nals.1682,1683

CSF Panels and Incre­men­tal Value: Two cohorts found cor­re­la­tions among CSF neu­ro­granin, SNAP-25, and PSD-95, strongest between neu­ro­granin and SNAP-25. Aver­age con­cen­tra­tions were ele­vated in Alzheimer dis­ease, but inflam­ma­tory and vas­cu­lar out­liers reduced speci­ficity. Incon­sis­tent lon­gi­tu­di­nal follow-up and cog­ni­tive test­ing left treat­ment-response mon­i­tor­ing unproven. A larger pro­teomics study of 3,397 people across six cohorts derived a YWHAG:NPTX2 ratio that added cog­ni­tive and prog­nos­tic infor­ma­tion beyond models already con­tain­ing neu­ro­granin and other mark­ers. Incre­men­tal value must there­fore be tested against exist­ing panels.1684,1685

Practical Considerations

Assay-Spe­cific Thresh­olds: Research thresh­olds are assay-spe­cific. One study derived 165.5 pg/mL for Alzheimer dis­ease versus non-neu­rode­gen­er­a­tive

neu­ro­log­i­cal dis­or­ders, with 93% sen­si­tiv­ity and 41% speci­ficity. Another com­mer­cial-ELISA study derived 319 pg/mL with 73% sen­si­tiv­ity and 73% speci­ficity in its pop­u­la­tion. Nei­ther value is a common clin­i­cal ref­er­ence range.1686,1687

Spec­i­men and Mol­e­c­u­lar Forms: CSF includes full-length neu­ro­granin, ter­mi­nal frag­ments, and larger mol­e­c­u­lar forms. C-ter­mi­nal frag­ments con­tributed roughly half the total-neu­ro­granin ELISA signal in one exper­i­ment, with­out a dif­fer­ence in frag­ment-to-total ratio between Alzheimer and con­trol groups. A paired-fluid study found higher CSF con­cen­tra­tion with­out a plasma group dif­fer­ence or CSF-plasma cor­re­la­tion. Spec­i­men and rec­og­nized mol­e­c­u­lar forms must accom­pany every result.1651,1688

Vesi­cle-Attri­bu­tion Uncer­tainty: Neu­ronal attri­bu­tion of blood vesi­cles remains con­tested. One study found L1CAM dis­so­ci­ated from extra­cel­lu­lar vesi­cles and advised against using it for neu­ronal iso­la­tion. A single-vesi­cle study detected neu­ronal pro­teins on L1CAM-pos­i­tive vesi­cles and sup­ported enrich­ment. Iso­la­tion, frac­tion­a­tion, and char­ac­ter­i­za­tion are there­fore part of the mea­sure­ment rather than assump­tions sup­plied by the marker name.1689,1690

Col­lec­tion and Stor­age: Col­lec­tion and stor­age also matter. A neu­ro­granin trunc P75 assay com­par­i­son found pro­ce­dure-depen­dent con­cen­tra­tion dif­fer­ences. The NACC work­group rec­om­mends val­i­dated low-bind­ing col­lec­tion tubes and val­i­dated tubes suit­able for -80°C when sam­ples will enter long-term biorepos­i­to­ries. Tube selec­tion, pro­cess­ing, and freeze-thaw his­tory should be con­sis­tent for lon­gi­tu­di­nal inter­pre­ta­tion.1691,1692

14Biomarker Escalation and Stopping Tree Framework

Biomarker Escalation and Stopping Tree Framework

A bio­marker can guide action only when its intended pur­pose, mea­sure­ment reli­a­bil­ity, and con­nec­tion to the pro­posed deci­sion are defined. One ana­lyte may

serve dif­fer­ent roles across pop­u­la­tions, spec­i­mens, and treat­ment set­tings. The sequence below is an evi­dence-appraisal frame­work, not a treat­ment pro­to­col or a val­i­dated numer­i­cal stop­ping rule.1693,1694

Evaluation Criteria

Spec­ify the Use and the Mea­sure­ment: First clas­sify the pro­posed role as sus­cep­ti­bil­ity or risk, diag­no­sis, mon­i­tor­ing, prog­no­sis, treat­ment pre­dic­tion,

phar­ma­co­dy­namic response, or safety. Then record the ana­lyte or phys­i­o­log­i­cal fea­ture, spec­i­men, assay, units, pop­u­la­tion, and mea­sure­ment window. BEST requires spec­i­men source and method for cross-lab­o­ra­tory inter­pre­ta­tion. A shared bio­marker name does not prove com­pa­ra­ble mea­sure­ment.1693,1695,1696

Sep­a­rate Prog­no­sis from Pre­dic­tion: Asso­ci­a­tion with pro­gres­sion can iden­tify higher-risk par­tic­i­pants with­out show­ing which treat­ment will help them. Treat­ment pre­dic­tion gen­er­ally needs com­par­isons between treat­ment and con­trol across bio­marker strata. Even a sta­tis­ti­cally cred­i­ble treat­ment-by-bio­marker inter­ac­tion may have little selec­tion value if ben­e­fit in both strata leads to the same treat­ment choice.1697

Estab­lish Fit­ness for Pur­pose: Ana­lyt­i­cal val­i­da­tion tests whether the assay mea­sures its intended target. Clin­i­cal val­i­da­tion tests whether that result rep­re­sents the rel­e­vant bio­log­i­cal or clin­i­cal con­cept. Evi­dence must scale with the deci­sion and its con­se­quences. A dis­cov­ery asso­ci­a­tion may sup­port enrich­ment research yet remain insuf­fi­cient for a sur­ro­gate end­point used to estab­lish clin­i­cal ben­e­fit.1698

Dis­tin­guish Activ­ity from Patient Ben­e­fit: A phar­ma­co­dy­namic change can show that an inter­ven­tion engages biol­ogy with­out demon­strat­ing improve­ment in sur­vival, func­tion, or symp­toms. BEST uses sweat chlo­ride during assess­ment of CFTR poten­tia­tors as a phar­ma­co­dy­namic exam­ple and HbA1c reduc­tion as a val­i­dated sur­ro­gate for dia­betes-related microvas­cu­lar com­pli­ca­tions. Both are con­text-spe­cific. Nei­ther licenses favor­able bio­marker change as a uni­ver­sal sur­ro­gate.1699

Eval­u­ate Safety Sep­a­rately: Safety bio­mark­ers con­cern the prob­a­bil­ity, pres­ence, or extent of harm. BEST exam­ples include hepatic amino­trans­ferases with biliru­bin for hepa­to­tox­i­c­ity and serum potas­sium during expo­sure to drugs that alter potas­sium bal­ance. Inter­pre­ta­tion depends on the expo­sure and clin­i­cal set­ting. A favor­able response marker cannot cancel a simul­ta­ne­ous safety signal, and a non­spe­cific change should be tested against other expla­na­tions before causal attri­bu­tion.1700

Use the Gov­ern­ing Action Rule: For an approved prod­uct, iden­tify the exact for­mu­la­tion and indi­ca­tion, then con­sult cur­rent pre­scrib­ing infor­ma­tion and safety updates for dose adjust­ment, con­traindi­ca­tions, warn­ings, and mon­i­tor­ing. FDA dis­tin­guishes latest approved Drugs@FDA label­ing from cur­rent in-use Dai­ly­Med or FDAL­a­bel text that may include changes under review. In research, the autho­rized pro­to­col sup­plies pre­spec­i­fied action thresh­olds. This frame­work sup­plies none of its own.1701

Interpretation and Use

FDA bio­marker qual­i­fi­ca­tion applies to a stated con­text of use in drug devel­op­ment. It does not itself clear or approve a patient-care diag­nos­tic test. FDA may also

con­sider bio­marker evi­dence within a par­tic­u­lar drug appli­ca­tion with­out sep­a­rate qual­i­fi­ca­tion. Qual­i­fi­ca­tion status, assay per­for­mance, and sup­port for a clin­i­cal action are there­fore inde­pen­dent ques­tions.1702

15GLP-1R and GIPR Pharmacogenomics and Tiered Actionability

GLP-1R and GIPR Pharmacogenomics and Tiered Actionability

Incretin phar­ma­coge­nomics asks whether genetic vari­a­tion explains dif­fer­ences in glu­cose low­er­ing, weight change, or tol­er­a­bil­ity. Recep­tor vari­ants inter­ro­gate

target biol­ogy, while vari­ants affect­ing recep­tor reg­u­la­tion or incretin metab­o­lism test other mech­a­nisms. Because these out­comes and path­ways differ, each asso­ci­a­tion needs inde­pen­dent val­i­da­tion and incre­men­tal-value test­ing.1703–1705

Applications

Glycemic Response: A genome-wide analy­sis pooled four prospec­tive obser­va­tional cohorts and two ran­dom­ized-trial pro­grams, total­ing 4,571 adults with type

2 dia­betes. Six-month HbA1c reduc­tion after start­ing a GLP-1 recep­tor ago­nist was 0.08 per­cent­age points smaller per GLP1R rs6923761 serine allele. An ARRB1 signal driven largely by Thr370Met cor­re­sponded to 0.25 per­cent­age points greater reduc­tion per methio­n­ine allele. Sim­i­lar ARRB1 effects appeared in His­panic and Amer­i­can Indian or Alaska Native par­tic­i­pants, among whom the vari­ant was more fre­quent. The study did not test geno­type-guided pre­scrib­ing, despite propos­ing ear­lier GLP-1 use in selected car­ri­ers.1703

Can­di­date Weight-Response Sig­nals: Small GLP1R rs6923761 stud­ies dis­agree. A ran­dom­ized liraglu­tide analy­sis found only a nom­i­nal body-fat trend (p = 0.062). A 90-patient liraglu­tide cohort reported greater anthro­po­met­ric improve­ment in A-allele car­ri­ers and called the result pre­lim­i­nary. Among 112 people with severe obe­sity receiv­ing semaglu­tide, only nine were AA homozy­gotes. Their mean monthly weight-loss rate was 1.64% versus 1.04% in G-allele car­ri­ers, with geno­type and sex retained as pre­dic­tors. These are dif­fer­ent treat­ment set­tings and end­points from the glycemic study.1706–1708

In another obser­va­tional com­par­i­son, 27 adults received met­formin or oral semaglu­tide, with only ten semaglu­tide users. Nom­i­nal rs6923761 trends in weight and body-mass index among those ten did not sur­vive false-dis­cov­ery-rate cor­rec­tion. A larger ret­ro­spec­tive cohort included 210 oral-semaglu­tide users fol­lowed for a median 18 months. Among the 123 par­tic­i­pants who con­sented to genetic analy­ses, nei­ther rs6923761 nor rs761387 was sig­nif­i­cantly asso­ci­ated with changes in HbA1c, body-mass index, or blood pres­sure. These null find­ings limit trans­fer of small-cohort sig­nals across pop­u­la­tions and for­mu­la­tions.1709,1710

Larger Weight-Response Analy­ses: A 2025 analy­sis cov­ered 10,960 people from nine biobanks: 6,750 GLP-1 recep­tor-ago­nist users and 4,210 bariatric-surgery patients. After cor­rec­tion for mul­ti­ple test­ing, selected body-mass-index and type 2 dia­betes poly­genic scores and tested coding vari­ants were not sig­nif­i­cantly asso­ci­ated with response in the drug group. The total cohort size should not be reported as the treated sample.1711

A 2026 survey col­lected infor­ma­tion from 27,885 med­ica­tion users. Its prin­ci­pal genome-wide weight-response analy­sis used 15,237 unre­lated par­tic­i­pants of Euro­pean ances­try with com­plete data. Each GLP1R rs10305420 T allele asso­ci­ated with about 0.76 kg addi­tional weight loss. The find­ing repli­cated in 4,855 All of Us par­tic­i­pants using elec­tronic-health-record data. UK Biobank repli­ca­tion was neg­a­tive but had low expected power. Ascer­tain­ment, drug, phe­no­type, and analy­sis-pop­u­la­tion dif­fer­ences qual­ify com­par­isons with ear­lier null stud­ies.1704

Tol­er­a­bil­ity: The same 2026 study asso­ci­ated GLP1R and GIPR vari­ants with nausea or vom­it­ing. The GIPR asso­ci­a­tion appeared only among tirzepatide users, and rs1800437 related to adverse effects with­out a weight-loss asso­ci­a­tion. Tol­er­a­bil­ity and effi­cacy are there­fore sep­a­rate poten­tial appli­ca­tions.1704

Incretin Phys­i­ol­ogy: DPP4 rs6741949 was eval­u­ated in 1,976 non­di­a­betic par­tic­i­pants, with incretin mea­sure­ments avail­able in 168. Its inter­ac­tion with body-fat con­tent asso­ci­ated with glu­cose-stim­u­lated GLP-1, insulin secre­tion, and glu­cose tol­er­ance. The authors explic­itly stated that drug response to DPP-4 inhi­bi­tion had not been tested, keep­ing phys­i­ol­ogy dis­tinct from phar­ma­coge­nomic effi­cacy.1705

Practical Considerations

Pre­dic­tive and Clin­i­cal Action­abil­ity: Cur­rent evi­dence ranges from pre­lim­i­nary can­di­date stud­ies to large asso­ci­a­tions with par­tial exter­nal repli­ca­tion. Drug,

dose, treat­ment dura­tion, sex, and other clin­i­cal fac­tors con­tributed to the 2026 response models, and self-reported dose and weight his­to­ries dif­fered from linked records. The unre­solved clin­i­cal ques­tions are whether geno­type adds useful pre­dic­tion beyond those fac­tors and whether acting on it improves out­comes. None of the sum­ma­rized stud­ies tested the latter ques­tion.1704,1703

16Peptide Stacking: Combination-Specific Evidence Gaps

Peptide Stacking: Combination-Specific Evidence Gaps

Pep­tide stack­ing refers here to con­cur­rent use of sep­a­rately sourced pep­tide prod­ucts. Evi­dence for each com­po­nent does not estab­lish the iden­tity, expo­sure,

phar­ma­co­ki­net­ics, inter­ac­tions, effi­cacy or safety of the com­plete reg­i­men. An inter­pretable claim there­fore begins with each mol­e­cule, for­mu­la­tion, route, sched­ule, com­para­tor and intended out­come.1712

Evaluation Criteria

Multi-Recep­tor Phar­ma­col­ogy: Tirzepatide illus­trates why one dual ago­nist cannot be recon­structed from recep­tor names. At clin­i­cally rel­e­vant mod­eled

expo­sures, the mol­e­cule engaged GIP recep­tors more strongly than GLP-1 recep­tors. At GLP-1 recep­tors it favored cAMP over beta-arrestin recruit­ment and caused less recep­tor inter­nal­iza­tion than GLP-1. Those inte­grated prop­er­ties belong to tirzepatide, not to any pair of inde­pen­dently admin­is­tered lig­ands.1713

Linked Deliv­ery Con­structs: A GLP-1-MK-801 con­ju­gate com­bined GLP-1-recep­tor ago­nism with NMDA-recep­tor antag­o­nism in rodent meta­bolic-dis­ease models. GLP-1-directed deliv­ery changed hypo­thal­a­mic and brain­stem responses and avoided adverse phys­i­o­log­i­cal and behav­ioral effects observed with MK-801 alone. This is evi­dence for one linked pre­clin­i­cal con­struct. It does not show that sep­a­rate com­po­nents repro­duce its dis­tri­b­u­tion or tol­er­a­bil­ity in humans.1560

Direct Com­bi­na­tion Research: A 2026 Achilles-tendon repair study ran­dom­ized 32 young male rats to con­trol, BPC-157, vendor-labelled TB-500 or both, eight per group. The abstract sum­ma­rizes intraperi­toneal treat­ment as four weeks. The full Meth­ods and figure spec­ify daily injec­tions for 30 post­op­er­a­tive days and day-30 har­vest, with BPC-157 10 micro­grams/kg/day and TB-500 60 micro­grams/kg/day. The full paper divided four ten­dons per group to bio­me­chan­i­cal test­ing and four to his­to­log­i­cal, his­to­chem­i­cal and immuno­his­to­chem­i­cal analy­sis. These source-spe­cific details should not be inferred from the abstract alone.1714

Only the TB-500 group dif­fered sig­nif­i­cantly from con­trol in max­i­mum load to fail­ure. Total Bonar score was lower only with TB-500, while total Movin score was lower with TB-500 and the com­bi­na­tion. The com­bi­na­tion was not supe­rior to either monother­apy. Pro­posed path­way con­ver­gence, ceil­ing effects or dose effects were not tested because the study lacked mol­e­c­u­lar and phar­ma­co­ki­netic analy­ses. It also used one time point, healthy young male rats, four ten­dons per end­point type and no func­tional out­come.1714

Reported Human Use: A ret­ro­spec­tive knee-pain report con­tacted 16 of 17 treated patients by tele­phone. Eleven of 12 who received intra-artic­u­lar BPC-157 alone and three of four who received BPC-157 plus thy­mosin-beta-4 reported improve­ment. Treat­ment was not ran­dom­ized, follow-up varied, and no stan­dard­ized func­tion, qual­ity-of-life, stiff­ness or daily-activ­ity instru­ment was used. The report doc­u­ments sub­jec­tive use. It cannot estab­lish effi­cacy, com­par­a­tive ben­e­fit or syn­ergy.18

Iden­tity And Prod­uct Attri­bu­tion: The rat paper iden­ti­fied cat­a­logue prod­ucts 223-018-PS and 27-155-PS from Pep­tide Sci­ences but did not pro­vide amino-acid sequences for the admin­is­tered mate­ri­als. It called TB-500 a syn­thetic pep­tide derived from thy­mosin beta-4 and selected its dose from broader thy­mosin-beta-4 lit­er­a­ture. The knee report named thy­mosin-beta-4. Nei­ther record jus­ti­fies treat­ing every mar­keted TB-500 prod­uct, frag­ment or full-length thy­mosin-beta-4 prepa­ra­tion as iden­ti­cal.1714.18

Prod­uct And Route Risks: FDA specif­i­cally flags com­pounded BPC-157 for poten­tial immuno­genic­ity with cer­tain routes, pep­tide-related impu­ri­ties and ingre­di­ent-char­ac­ter­i­za­tion chal­lenges, with no or lim­ited safety infor­ma­tion for pro­posed routes. Its list­ing for thy­mosin beta-4 frag­ment (LKK­TETQ), called TB-500 there, flags poten­tial aggre­ga­tion and pep­tide-related impu­ri­ties and says FDA has iden­ti­fied no human expo­sure data for drug prod­ucts con­tain­ing that frag­ment. The FDA list­ing does not estab­lish that the frag­ment is iden­ti­cal to the unse­quenced rat-study mate­r­ial or to the thy­mosin-beta-4 prepa­ra­tion named in the knee report. FDA also dis­tin­guishes some routes, such as injectable GHK-Cu from other forms. These records do not estab­lish one risk pro­file for all pep­tides, and com­bin­ing prod­ucts com­pli­cates attri­bu­tion with­out resolv­ing their iden­tity or qual­ity.1715.1716

Interpretation and Use

A com­bi­na­tion-spe­cific ben­e­fit requires the com­plete reg­i­men to out­per­form appro­pri­ate com­po­nent and con­trol groups on rel­e­vant out­comes. Mech­a­nis­tic

plau­si­bil­ity or improve­ment from each com­po­nent in sep­a­rate stud­ies does not estab­lish addi­tiv­ity, syn­ergy or a favor­able inter­ac­tion. Simul­ta­ne­ous changes in sev­eral expo­sures also make adverse-event attri­bu­tion harder.1712.1714

The avail­able exam­ples span a mol­e­cule with inte­grated dual-recep­tor phar­ma­col­ogy, a linked tar­get­ing con­struct, one small animal com­bi­na­tion study and one uncon­trolled ret­ro­spec­tive report. They answer dif­fer­ent ques­tions and cannot be pooled into a gen­eral rec­om­men­da­tion for pep­tide stack­ing. Human phar­ma­co­ki­net­ics, clin­i­cally rel­e­vant end­points and com­bi­na­tion-spe­cific safety remain unre­solved for the BPC-157 and TB-500 reg­i­men described here.1713.1560,1714,18

17FDA 503A Category 2 Regulatory Reference

FDA 503A Category 2 Regulatory Reference

US com­pound­ing records dis­tin­guish statu­tory con­di­tions, interim nom­i­na­tion cat­e­gories, sub­stance-spe­cific safety assess­ments, and advi­sory-com­mit­tee

review. Every inter­pre­ta­tion should pre­serve record date, chem­i­cal iden­tity, route, and whether sec­tion 503A or 503B applies. The FDA mate­ri­als sum­ma­rized here were checked on 2026-09-10.1259,1717,1718

Scope and Requirements

Statu­tory Path­way: For bulk sub­stances under sec­tion 503A, FDA describes an ordered set of con­di­tions. A sub­stance with an applic­a­ble USP or NF mono­graph

must comply with it and with the USP phar­macy-com­pound­ing chap­ter. With­out such a mono­graph, the sub­stance must either be a com­po­nent of an approved drug or appear on the final 503A bulks list. Cer­tifi­cate-of-analy­sis and estab­lish­ment-reg­is­tra­tion require­ments also apply. Interim nom­i­na­tion status does not estab­lish that a fin­ished com­pounded prod­uct sat­is­fies all statu­tory con­di­tions.1718,1719

Mean­ing of Cat­e­gory 2: Cat­e­gory 2 con­tains nom­i­nated sub­stances for which FDA iden­ti­fied sig­nif­i­cant safety risks while eval­u­a­tion remains pend­ing. These sub­stances are out­side the interim enforce­ment policy described for Cat­e­gory 1, and FDA says it would con­sider action under gen­eral enforce­ment poli­cies. Cat­e­gory 2 is not approval and does not inde­pen­dently decide every use, route, for­mu­la­tion, or legal ques­tion.1718

Dated Sub­stance Records: The 503A cat­e­gory doc­u­ment remained dated 2026-05-14 when checked on 2026-09-10. Active Cat­e­gory 2 entries were cesium chlo­ride, dom­peri­done, ger­ma­nium sesquiox­ide, ibu­ta­moren mesy­late, kisspeptin-10, and quinacrine hydrochlo­ride for intrauter­ine admin­is­tra­tion. Kisspeptin-10 was the only pep­tide in that dated active list. Sep­a­rate FDA safety sum­maries remained avail­able for with­drawn nom­i­na­tions includ­ing AOD-9604, BPC-157, LL-37, CJC-1295, epi­talon, injectable GHK-Cu, KPV, MOTS-c, semax, and TB-500. With­drawal is nei­ther cur­rent Cat­e­gory 2 status nor a safety find­ing by itself.1259,1717

Route and Statu­tory Sec­tion: FDA’s 2026-05-14 update returned GHK-Cu, except injectable routes, to Cat­e­gory 1 after the nom­i­na­tor clar­i­fied the with­drawal scope. This does not estab­lish eli­gi­bil­ity for injectable GHK-Cu. Ipamore­lin acetate appears on the with­drawn-nom­i­na­tion safety page while remain­ing in Cat­e­gory 2 under the sep­a­rate 503B interim policy. Sub­stance name alone can there­fore mis­state the gov­ern­ing record.1259,1717

Advi­sory Process: The 2026-07-23 and 2026-07-24 meet­ing record listed BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, emideltide, semax, and epi­talon for con­sid­er­a­tion, iden­ti­fy­ing free-base and acetate forms and the eval­u­ated uses. FDA’s brief­ing states that final deter­mi­na­tion fol­lows com­mit­tee input and com­ple­tion of agency review. A non­bind­ing rec­om­men­da­tion, agenda, or brief­ing pack­age is not a final bulks-list deci­sion or approval of a fin­ished drug.1720,1719

Practical Implications

FDA states that nom­i­na­tions sub­mit­ted on or after 2025-01-07 will not enter the interim cat­e­gories, although suf­fi­ciently sup­ported nom­i­na­tions can still undergo

final-list eval­u­a­tion. Absence from an interim cat­e­gory there­fore does not settle eli­gi­bil­ity. A present-day pre­scrib­ing, com­pound­ing, or legal deci­sion requires the cur­rent sub­stance and route record, applic­a­ble statu­tory path­way, and any later final action.1718
