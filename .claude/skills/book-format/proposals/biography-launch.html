2.1 Why changes stop being frightening

What normally happens

A typesetter reopens the file, moves text by hand, and every page after the change shifts. It takes days, it costs money, and it is why publishers stop accepting edits months before print.

In traditional production, a late correction is expensive. Someone has to reflow the pages by hand, and everything downstream of the change moves — page numbers, the contents, the index, every cross-reference. That is why you are normally told the manuscript is locked long before you feel ready.

Here, a corrected chapter is simply dropped back in and the whole book rebuilds itself. Contents, index, footnote numbering and cross-references all recalculate. It takes minutes, and it costs nothing.

The practical effect is that we can keep improving the book right up to the print deadline instead of freezing it early. On {{schedule.weeksWithArticle}} schedule that is not a convenience — it is the reason the schedule is possible at all.

2.2 Nothing reaches you half-checked

Before any proof comes to you, it is checked automatically for the mechanical faults that plague long documents: text running off the page, images too low-resolution to print cleanly, chapters starting on the wrong side, a single line stranded at the top or bottom of a page, a heading left dangling with its section overleaf, a cross-reference pointing at nothing.

Those are found and fixed before you see the pages. Your attention goes to the words and the science, which is the only part a machine cannot judge. You will never be asked to proofread for typesetting errors — only to read for content.

3.1 The editorial process

None of the above is improvised. Every {{publisher.name}} book goes through the same editorial process, and it exists because a transcript is not a book — a recorded conversation is loose, circles back on itself, and assumes things the reader has not been told yet. The process is what closes that gap, and it is built to produce four things.

  1. A comprehensive book. The developmental pass reads the whole thing as a reader will and finds what is missing rather than what is wrong — the step you skipped because it is obvious to you, the term introduced forty pages after it was first used, the argument that needs one more example to land. That gap list comes back to you as questions, and the answers are recorded like any other interview.
  2. Your voice, perfected. The draft is checked against the recordings, not against a style guide. Where a sentence reads like a writer rather than like you, it goes back. You approve every chapter in your own voice before it is composed, which is the only test that matters.
  3. A reader drawn into your world. The order of a book is not the order the interviews happen in. The structural pass sequences the material so a reader who knows nothing arrives at each idea already holding what they need for it — which is why chapters open on a person and not a definition.
  4. The transformation delivered. The promise in §1.1 is treated as a specification rather than a blurb. Each section is read back against it: does the reader leave this one able to do something they could not do before it. A section that does not earn its place is rewritten or cut.

Then the ordinary craft — copy editing and proofreading — after the book is right rather than before, because polishing prose that is about to be restructured is work nobody pays twice for.
