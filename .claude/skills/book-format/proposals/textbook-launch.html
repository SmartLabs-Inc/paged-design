2.1 Why changes stop being frightening

What normally happens

A typesetter reopens the file, moves text by hand, and every page after the change shifts. It takes days, it costs money, and it is why publishers stop accepting edits months before print.

In traditional production, a late correction is expensive. Someone has to reflow the pages by hand, and everything downstream of the change moves — page numbers, the contents, the index, every cross-reference. That is why you are normally told the manuscript is locked long before you feel ready.

Here, a corrected chapter is simply dropped back in and the whole book rebuilds itself. Contents, index, footnote numbering and cross-references all recalculate. It takes minutes, and it costs nothing.

The practical effect is that we can keep improving the book right up to the print deadline instead of freezing it early. On {{schedule.weeksWithArticle}} schedule that is not a convenience — it is the reason the schedule is possible at all.

2.2 Nothing reaches you half-checked

Before any proof comes to you, it is checked automatically for the mechanical faults that plague long documents: text running off the page, images too low-resolution to print cleanly, chapters starting on the wrong side, a single line stranded at the top or bottom of a page, a heading left dangling with its section overleaf, a cross-reference pointing at nothing.

Those are found and fixed before you see the pages. Your attention goes to the words and the science, which is the only part a machine cannot judge. You will never be asked to proofread for typesetting errors — only to read for content.

Section 03

Making It Human

The chapters are correct. They are also cold.

This is the most important section in this proposal, and the work that will do the most for the book’s reception.

Starting immediately

Interviews begin in Week 1, before design is even locked. This work sits on the critical path and cannot be compressed later.

The manuscript as it stands reads like a reference document. It is accurate, thorough, and utterly without a pulse. A reader learns from it but never feels that a person is speaking to them — and readers do not recommend books that did not move them.

A textbook can carry warmth without losing rigor. The science stays exactly as precise as it is now; what changes is that a human being becomes audible in it.

How we do it

I will interview you, starting immediately. Not about the science you have already written down — about the patients, the moments the evidence changed your mind, the thing you believed in your first year of practice that you no longer believe, the case that still bothers you.

Those interviews become ghostwritten stories, and those stories are woven through the chapters: a chapter opens on a patient rather than a definition, a mechanism is anchored to the moment you first watched it play out, a summary closes on why it matters to someone in your chair.

The result

A textbook a clinician reads for the science, and remembers because of the story it was carried in.

What this requires from you

Time, and candor. A handful of recorded conversations in the first two weeks. You do not write anything; you talk, and it comes back to you as prose in your own voice for approval.

4.2 Presentation and on-camera setup

Let me be precise about this, because it is a much smaller problem than people usually assume when they hear “media training.”

You are good in an interview. You speak well, you handle a host, you answer the question. None of that needs fixing.

What undercuts you is entirely physical, and every piece of it is a one-time fix:

  1. Your backdrop. Whatever happens to be behind you, rather than something chosen. A producer reads it before they hear a word.
  2. Your lighting. Currently accidental. Correct lighting is one lamp, placed once, and it is the single biggest difference between looking like a guest and looking like an expert.
  3. What you wear. No attention paid to it at present. This needs a decided-once wardrobe that reads as authority on camera and never has to be thought about again.
  4. Where your head sits in the frame. Anywhere but where it should be. Camera height, distance, and eyeline are three numbers you set once and mark with tape.

What this actually takes

One session to set the room, the light, the camera and the frame — and a photograph of the finished setup so it can be rebuilt in thirty seconds. A second session on camera to confirm it holds under a real interview.

After that, the thing an audience sees matches the thing you know. That is the whole gap.

7.3 Patient material

Clinical stories are what will make this book readable, and they are also the material most likely to cause harm if handled carelessly. The rule we work to is narrower than the law requires, on purpose.

We write the symptomology, never the patient. What appears on the page is the presentation, the findings, the reasoning, and the outcome. What does not appear is anything that could walk a reader back to a person.

  1. Age is given by decade only — "a man in his sixties," never a number.
  2. Ethnicity is omitted unless it is genetically material to the case, in which case it is stated as the genetic fact it is and nothing more.
  3. Occupation, location, family detail and timeline are generalized or dropped wherever they narrow the field.
  4. Composites are labeled as composites, so no reader mistakes an illustration for a single case.

Where a case genuinely requires identifying detail to make its point, it runs only with written authorization you provide. We will not publish an identifiable patient on a verbal assurance, and this is one of the few places in this engagement where we will hold the schedule rather than proceed.
